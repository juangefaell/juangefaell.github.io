History and philosophy of biology

I love science. By that, I not only mean that I enjoy learning about what science reveals about the world, but also that I’m fascinated by the scientific approach to knowledge. As humans, it’s not always easy for us to think rationally, as we are often prone to biases of various kinds. Science—understood as a set of institutions and habits of inquiry—seeks to mitigate those biases so that we can reach sound conclusions about how the world works.

Because I love science, I want to understand how it works. This is why I study the history and philosophy of science. More specifically, my work focuses on conceptual issues surrounding evolutionary biology—a particularly salient scientific discipline. Much like biologists study specific organisms to understand how life works, I investigate evolutionary biology to gain insights into how science operates. For this, I use different approaches, but I’m increasingly drawn to computational, bibliometric and empirical methods in philosophy.

In the last few years, I have mostly worked on scientific disagreement and inter-theory relationships in the context of the EES debate, not only seeking to understand the debate, but also to improve the way it unfolds. During my postdoc at Lund University under the supervision of Tobias Uller, I am studying how novel ideas are received in evolutionary biology using computational and bibliometric techniques. Specifically, our main project involves assessing the historical impact of Mary Jane West-Eberhard’s plasticity-first hypothesis on evolutionary discussions. By analyzing how the evolutionary biology community assimilates disruptive ideas we can gain insights into how scientific disciplines change over time.

Evolutionary biology

In addition to the history and philosophy of biology, I also conduct research in evolutionary biology, using ecological, ethological, and genetical insights to understand how natural selection and other evolutionary mechanisms maintain adaptive phenotypic diversity within and across populations. My PhD work focused on the maintenance mechanisms of color variation in the marine gastropod L. saxatilis. Currently, I collaborate with Emilio Rolán-Alvarez and Juan Galindo, my former PhD supervisors at the University of Vigo, in the context of their EVOLPRED project, which is aimed at understanding the predictability of adaptive evolution. Doing research in evolutionary biology does not only allow me to study fascinating natural history questions but also to gain first-hand experience of how science actually works.

Science education

Finally, from time to time I participate in research on science education, also in close collaboration with Emilio Rolán-Alvarez. Currently, for example, we’re studying whether university students’ trust in science is based on a fair portrayal of the nature of science. Research in science education does not only contribute to designing more effective scientific literacy policies, but it can also inform us about people’s folk philosophy of science.
