An Optimistic Take on Meaning: A Review of Iddo Landau's "Finding Meaning in an Imperfect World" (OUP, 2017)

This week I finished a fascinating book on the philosophy of meaning in life: Finding Meaning in an Imperfect World, by Iddo Landau, Professor of Philosophy in Haifa University (Israel). I came across this book a few months ago surfing on the internet, and I found it intriguing because it seemed to present an optimistic yet balanced defense of the thesis that there is indeed meaning in life, a thesis with which I tend to agree with but that, at least to my knowledge, it is not very popular among (secular) philosophers working on this topic, or at least among mainstream philosophers, such as the Schopenhauers, Kierkegaards and Nietzsches of the world[1].

So I decided to buy the book and read it right away. And I immediately fell in love with it. It is the kind of book that I would want to write on the topic of meaning in life. Most of the time, Landau acts as a sort of cognitive-behavioral therapist, offering rebuttals to all cognitive distortions that usually accompany many popular and philosophical ideas about the meaninglessness of life. In fact, I believe that the book is so successful in doing so that the only thing that makes me sad is that it is not available in other languages so that psychotherapists dealing with patients with these kinds of existential problems outside the English-speaking world cannot make use of the arguments put forward by Landau. Besides, the book is so clearly written that it is a pleasure to read and fairly easy to catch Landau’s ideas.

Let me briefly describe the things that I liked the most about this wonderful book. Of course, the following does not pretend to be a systematic summary of the book, but only a personal selection of the points that I found more useful or interesting. 

1.- Operationalizing meaning. Usually, discussions about the meaning of life tend to be quite obscure, abstract and unintelligible. At least in my experience, whenever someone talks about the meaning of life, he does so as if it were some kind of mystical or unknowable thing. Right from the introduction of the book, Landau advocates a down-to-earth and operational conception of meaning, successfully arguing that most discussions about meaning can be translated into discussions of worth and value without any theoretical loss. Although it could be argued that the concepts of “worth” and “value” are neither very concrete or tangible, they are nonetheless much more tractable that the concept of meaning. Operationalizing meaning in the way Landau does helps dealing with discussions of meaninglessness in a much more manageable fashion, also helping rebut some commonly-held assumptions about it that are plainly wrong when viewed in light of this meaning-as-value conception. 

2.- Avoid perfectionism about the meaning in life. Perhaps the main point of Landau’s book is that perfectionism is not only wrong, but it is also highly counterproductive. Applied to the meaning of life question, perfectionism is the view according to which, given that there is probably no absolute meaning to life, then it there is no meaning at all. Just as cognitive-behavioral therapists try to show patients the pitfalls of perfectionist thinking, and urge them to abandon such dichotomous way of thinking about life and replace it with a more subtle and nuanced way of approaching things, Landau recommends us to avoid perfectionism about meaning in life and embrace a less black or white position on it. The core of Landau’s rebuttal of many arguments for the meaninglessness of life consist of a criticism of perfectionism. For instance, many people argue that there is no meaning to life because we are mortal beings and our impact on the grand scheme of things is negligible. To this argument, Landau replies that maybe the fact that we are mortal somehow diminishes our meaning to life, but in no way cancels it completely. Life is meaningful when it includes a sufficient number of worthy things, and not when it satisfies a rather perfect or absolute criterion of meaningfulness. In short, Landau invites to think about meaning not as a matter of all or nothing, but as a matter of more or less (see figure 1).

3.- Meaning in life is not a given, but we can do something about it. A third important point of Landau’s Finding meaning in an imperfect world is that discussions about meaning in life sometimes tacitly assume that this is a static and fixed feature of life: we either do or do not have a meaningful life. And there is nothing we can do to change the way things are. Landau challenges this assumption, arguing instead that, although our mental and material capacities are certainly limited, we have the power to increase the meaning in our lives to a significant degree. Landau suggests that we can increase the meaning of our life either by changing the way we think about it, trying to recognize the valuable things it contains, or by changing our behavior so that we seek for new sources of meaning to add to our lives. In this sense, Landau’s book urges us not to settle for the current state of affairs, and to do whatever is possible to improve our situation regarding the meaning of our lives.

Rebutting Existentialism

Besides those three points, one further thing that I found really interesting about Landau’s book is his criticism of existentialism. Existentialism is one of the most popular philosophies when it comes to discussions on the meaning of life. In my experience, it is a particularly popular philosophy among those philosophically-oriented folks who nonetheless do not have any formal training in philosophy. However, as Landau points out, existentialism not only does not provide satisfactory answers to the meaning of life question, but it may in fact contribute to diminishing our meaning in life by way of wrong premises and weak arguments. Landau identifies at least four major features of existentialism that may contribute to make things worst in relation to meaning in life. 

First, most existentialist philosophers are staunch perfectionists. Take, for instance, Nietzsche’s Übermensch; only this flawless and more than human figure would lead a meaningful life, while the rest of us –poor mortals– would be nothing but plain failures. In the long run, this ideology only breeds frustration and neglect of the more ordinary sources of meaning.

Second, many existentialists explicitly assume that life is irremediably absurd or meaningless, and that the only thing we can do is to try to cope as better as we can with this situation. But as Landau shows, this claim is based on poor arguments that do not stand up to scrutiny. Especially interesting is Landau’s critique of the analogy between human life and the myth of Sisyphus, so compelling for existentialist thinkers yet so poorly warranted. 

Third, most existentialist philosophers tend to ignore the good things in life, and present life as it were only suffering and pain. Furthermore, because of this emphasis on pain and suffering, they consider themselves as realist, truthful people who do not refuse to see the bad things in life. However, in their insistence that life is only suffering, they are as biased as the naïve individual who thinks that life is all joy and happiness. It may be that for some people at some moments in their life all, or almost all things involve suffering; but this is certainly not the case for most people most of the time. Life, for most people, tends to involve as many good things as bad, and focusing only on the bad things is not a sign of a realist attitude but of an unproductive cognitive bias. 

Finally, most existentialist thinkers tend to abhor society and community, equating them with herds and sanctifying the individual who breaks all ties with her peers. While breaking ties with society can sometimes be liberating, especially when society is made up of narrow-minded and/or oppressive individuals, existentialist thinking neglects the fact that, for most people, social relationships are a deep and enduring source of meaning. Therefore, by commanding people to withdraw from society, they are probably making things much worse. 

In sum, Finding meaning in an imperfect world is a wonderful book on a topic as interesting and important as the meaning of life, convincingly advancing the view that most arguments for the meaninglessness of life do not work once we operationalize meaning and abandon perfectionism and other false and unproductive philosophical positions that abound in this literature. The only criticism I would make to Landau is not having engaged with the psychological literature when defending his views. I think Landau would have found plenty of empirical data to support his arguments there. In any case, this is a fantastic book and I urge those interested in the philosophy of meaning in life to read it. 

P.D.: for those interested in Landau’s book, but who do not have the time or energy to read it, I strongly recommend his blog on Psychology Today. Full of short interesting posts on this and related topics!
