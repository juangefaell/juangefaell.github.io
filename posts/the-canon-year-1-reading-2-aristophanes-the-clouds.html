The Canon, year 1/reading 2—Aristophanes’ The Clouds

[NOTE: The first reading of the list is Plato's Apology of Socrates, but I had already read it, so I skipped it and went straight to the second, which is the one discussed below.]

This week, I read Aristophanes’ The Clouds. This comedy is of special significance to all those interested in philosophy, since it revolves around—and seeks to satirize—Socrates. In it, a desperate father named Strepsiades desperately looks for guiles that can help him avoid his debts, which he has contracted because of his irresponsible wife and, especially, son, Pheidippides, who spends extraordinary sums of money on equitation. After seeking solutions while awake at night, he tries to convince Pheidippides to learn the ways of the sophists, and particularly, of Socrates, so that he can use their rhetorical tricks against his creditors. 

Having failed to convince Pheidippides, Strepsiades himself joins Socrates’ Phrontisterion, his intellectual workshop. Aristophanes’ depiction of Socrates is telling: when Strepsiades gets into his house, he discovers that Socrates and their pupils are wasting time studying what seem to be pointless questions about the natural world, such as how many times can a flea jump its body. Socrates is represented as hanging from a rope, which he explains is a way of being closer to the sky so that his thoughts do not get spoiled by contact with earth and earthly matters. After explaining to Strepsiades all they do in the Phrontisterion, Socrates tells him that the true gods are not those of Olympus, but the Clouds, who are the ones responsible for all meteorological phenomena. 

Socrates manages to turn Strepsiades into a follower of the Clouds, while Strepsiades finally convinces his son Phidippides to join Socrates. But this time, Socrates, instead of teaching Phidippides the deceitful ways of rhetoric, invites him to listen to the “Worst Argument”, an impersonation of all the sophists’ intellectual vices. The Worst Argument, a critic of tradition and classical education, starts an argumentative fight with the “Best Argument”, its defender, which resolves in favor of the former. Once the fight is over, Strepsiades, who was also a witness, starts to apply the Worst Argument’s deceptive tactics against their creditors, effectively getting rid of his debts. But rightly afterwards, things take a wrong turn for him: his son Phidippides, now as well under the influence of the Worst Argument, starts kicking and beating his father, while sagaciously refuting all of the arguments Strepsiades puts forward to convince him to stop. 

Finally, in rage, Strepsiades realizes how perverse the ways of rhetoric are, and starts to chase Socrates and his pupils to beat them up. The comedy finishes with all of them running away from Strepsiades, and the Phrontisterion burning up. 

The Clouds is an interesting reading, if any, because Aristophanes’ depiction of Socrates as a fraudster sophist resonates with the accusations against him laid out in Plato’s Apology. In the Apology, Socrates states that he has been accused of corrupting the youth and of introducing new gods, as well as of devoting most of his intellectual efforts to astronomy and the natural sciences. Of course, to anyone who has read Plato’s dialogues, these accusations are false: Socrates barely wonders about nature, but rather about justice and ethics; and he neither introduces any god, although his insistence on critical arguments over blind tradition might have been understood by some as a question of the traditional deities. 

 Be that as it may, the accusation against Socrates seems to have either been influenced by Aristophanes’ depiction or Aristophanes was just putting into words a common perception of Socrates at the time of his trial. While such a perception might be false and quite offensive, it was apparently the case that neither Socrates nor Plato took it personally, and that they didn’t harbor any personal resentment toward either Aristophanes or The Clouds (Konstan, 2011). Otherwise, it is not understood, for instance, that Aristophanes features as an esteemed character in Plato’s Symposium. 

References
