The Canon, year 1/reading 8—The New Testament, Gospel of Saint Matthew

This last month I’ve been reading the New Testament, specifically, the Gospel of Saint Matthew. Despite having been brought up in an essentially Christian culture, this was my first mature interaction with a biblical text. Apart from the many expressions and sayings that nowadays are part of standard Spanish, and whose origin lies in fragments of the Bible—something which was interesting to learn—I should say that I’m quite disappointed with this reading. I’m in general skeptical of religion-based moralities, and of Christian ethics in particular, but I had been repeatedly told that the New Testament represented a moral advance when compared to—what in my view are—the scarcely moral, or even outright immoral, teachings of the Old Testament. While I concede that the Gospel of Saint Matthew contains some valuable ethical insights (particularly, those expressed in the Sermon on the Mount), I found a great deal of the text hard to swallow for anyone drawn to a contemporary, philosophy-based ethics, such as myself. 

I have several quarrels with the Gospel. First, a good part of it is devoted to the miracles of Jesus, which are introduced to highlight his divine nature. These miracles, in turn, are meant to strengthen his moral authority. The miracles are entertaining, but of course, they are hard to believe. Furthermore, I find them largely irrelevant to morality: unless you understand ethical consent as stemming from power rather than rational arguments and good will, why would you grant moral authority to someone who can perform miracles? In that respect, I cannot help but compare Jesus with Socrates. Socrates did not do any miracle, yet he seems to me much more morally inspiring than Jesus. Why? Because he not only stood up for his principles, but he also tried to provide cogent arguments to support them. We could hardly say the same of Jesus, who did not have the habit of providing reasons for accepting his claims, trying instead to persuade listeners with miracles, proclamations, and (to my taste obscure) parables.

As said, I view the morality of the Gospel as centered around power. Only assuming this I can make sense of the fact that so many of Jesus’ moral teachings concern obedience rather than compassion—one of the main messages of the Gospel is that people must obey God. Why we should do so is not clear, except because God is powerful and He loves being worshiped. Although the truth is that the Gospel gives us have an extra reason for obedience: if we are loyal, God can reward us. Actually, this is the closest thing to an argument that the Gospel puts forward: we should follow God and be moral (a morality that involves gender oppression and, at times, some degree of ethnocentrism, for that matter), because if we are, we will be rewarded with a blissful eternal existence. 

Which brings me to the next reason why I am disappointed with the Gospel and with (at least early) Christianity at large: a crucial motivation for being moral seems to be not that people have the right to be treated decently (as in modern ethical traditions), nor to develop humanity to the fullest and find joy in this life (as in most Classical Greek schools). Rather, individuals should be moral to eventually be awarded eternal life. This might be rational in a strict utility function sense but can hardly count as a moral motivation.  

Finally, another thing that I found very difficult to digest is Jesus’ fixation on those he does not like—particularly, the Pharisees and Sadducees. I don’t know how bad they were to him, but many of his condemnations are a bit exaggerated. For example, in several passages, he calls them “brood of vipers”, which is in clear contradiction to many of his most famous proclaims, including that of turning the other cheek. For me, the extreme belligerence against his enemies taints the little that I found praiseworthy of Jesus’ conduct and preaching. 

In sum, I find the Gospel of Saint Matthew a rather dubious moral guide. In my view, the text is clearly the product of an archaic epoch, full of superstitions, scarcity, and unjust social stereotypes, which prevents me from relating to it in any meaningful sense. In response to similar complaints, supporters often say that, precisely, the Bible is the product of its time, and that we should not judge it according to our own modern standards. I understand this; yet I see two problems with this argument. The first is that, by the time the New Testament was written, there were already works that put forward a much more compassionate and reasonable morality than that of Jesus. About the same time as the Gospel of Saint Matthew was written, for instance, Seneca published his Letters to Lucilius, which, to me, stands far above in sophistication and moral content than any biblical text I’ve read. This suggests that the Bible is not only the product of its time, but also of a particularly dogmatic and unreasonable moral and intellectual tradition. Second, many people nowadays still take the New Testament as a source of moral inspiration, which assumes that it still has something valuable to teach to contemporary humans. In other words, many people are implicitly treating the Bible as an atemporal source of moral guidance. I grant that the Bible might have something to teach us, but for each valuable moral insight, present-day readers have to swallow at least one bitter pill. Personally, I find this very difficult to handle.
