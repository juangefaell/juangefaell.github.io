On Properties

Few other concepts are more important in science and metaphysics than that of a property: Scientists characterize objects based on their properties, while metaphysicians quarrel about what exactly properties are—i.e., what their ontic status is. 

There is no single way to define a property, but if I were pressed to provide a definition—one anchored on a naturalistic worldview—this is what I would say:

PropertyDf: Feature or characteristic of a material entity. 

This definition seems pretty straightforward, but it contains more substance than might appear at first glance. 

First, according to the above definition, properties are characteristic of material entities, which makes the concept of a property subsidiary to the idea of a material entity. This entails that there cannot be properties in themselves but only “in” material objects. For example, there cannot be mass or shape without being substantiated in a particular thing. Thus, my view of reality, much like Aristotle’s or Bunge’s (Bunge, 1977; Aristotle, 2016), is ultimately based on the notion of material things—these are the primary constituents on which anything else, including properties, depends. Note: Material entities range from subatomic particles to human and animal societies, which is why the properties of material objects are varied (see more below). 

Second, the fact that, according to the above definition, properties are characteristic of material entities implies that, properly speaking, formal entities, such as theories, propositions, or concepts, do not have properties proper—or at least don’t have the same kind of properties as material objects have. Formal properties such as coherence, cognitive salience, or extension are of a fundamentally different nature from material properties, which is what ultimately the focus of a naturalistic metaphysics should be. Most importantly, formal properties do not exist “out there” but only in the objects that we humans create (ideas, concepts, models, theories, and so on). From this can be deduced that it’s a categorical mistake to attribute formal properties to material entities, like dialectical materialists do when they argue that an object is “contradictory”. Likewise, it’s incorrect to attribute material properties to formal objects, such as fragility, weight, or shape. 

Third, and related to the previous observation, the fact that properties are said of material objects means that they can be expressed in propositions through attributes. The structure of propositions involves a subject and a predicate, and such a predicate can consist of asserting an attribute of a specific object (e.g., the dog is black).  

Fourth, although reliant on the concept of a material entity, the above definition grants properties a crucial role in characterizing material entities. Basically, to describe a material object is to enumerate its properties; to identify it is to diagnose certain properties that allow it to be subsumed into a natural kind. For instance, to identify a particle as a hydrogen atom is to recognize that it has Z = 1 and an atomic mass of 1.0079 Da (both defining properties of atoms). Additionally, explaining the behavior of an object involves, to a good extent, revealing how certain properties relate to one another. For example, how pressure relates to temperature in a gas, or effective population size to genetic drift. 

Kinds of properties

I have enumerated four important implications of the above definition. Now, one might wonder what kinds of properties there are, since even an informal glance at the world reveals a staggering diversity of features that material entities can have. I have come up with the following list of properties, which can serve as a first approximation to characterizing properties:

-Related to their degree of fundamentality: This allows us to distinguish between essential and subordinate properties. Essential properties allow the establishment of natural classes and the ascription of individual objects to them. Subordinate properties, by contrast, are those that an individual might lose without changing their nature. Subordinate properties may help characterize specific subjects, but do not suffice to ascribe them to natural classes. For instance, a Littorina saxatilis individual can have lines or not, but that wouldn’t affect its belonging to the species Littorina saxatilis. This entails that the presence of lines is a subordinate property. Conversely, ovoviviparity is a defining feature of this species, meaning that the ascription of individuals to this biological class relies on them having this particular property. Likewise, having Z = 1 is an essential property of hydrogen atoms, while being in a water molecule or methane (CH4) is subordinate. Note: I’m aware of how problematic essential properties for species are in light of evolution; for the moment, let me point out that I understand a species’ essential nature as a temporally bounded homeostatic property cluster, drawing from Richard Boyd’s characterization (Boyd, 1989) but emphasizing its temporal dimension. 

-Related to their potentiality: This distinction separates properties that are already present in the object (actual) from those that are not but could be if the right conditions ensue. The actual vs. potency difference, already formulated by Aristotle, allows us to attribute to embryos, for instance, properties that they might eventually develop if ontogeny follows its expected course. An acorn seed can be said to possess the properties of an acorn tree in potency, but not in actuality. This distinction, though, can be applied to non-living beings, such as when it is said of an object that it is inflammable. 

-Related to their relationship with a percipient being: This criterion distinguishes between what John Locke called primary and secondary qualities or properties (Locke, 1997). Primary properties would be those intrinsic to the being in question, whereas secondary properties would be those that emerge from the interaction between the object and a percipient being. The classic example of a secondary property would be color; color is not a feature of objects themselves but emerges in the brains of certain organisms when exposed to reflected electromagnetic waves coming from such objects. Much like the relationship between material entities and properties, where the existence of the latter depends on the existence of the former, secondary properties are ontologically subordinate to primary properties—they could not exist if there were no primary properties. Scientific inquiry on secondary properties involves explaining how certain primary properties are experienced as secondary properties by certain percipient beings and under certain conditions, normally by resorting to different neural and environmental processes.

-Related to their ontological level: Properties can also vary depending on the layer of reality to which they belong. The material world is structured in levels of increasing complexity. Material entities assemble into aggregates, which leads to the appearance of emergent properties—properties that are not present in the constituents of the newly formed system, but exist in the system as a whole. Based on this criterion, we can at least distinguish between physical, chemical, biological, psychological, and sociological properties, each of which having its own sublevels of organization. This distinction is thus anchored on a basic feature of material systems—their aggregative nature and the qualitative novelty that results from it. 

-Related to their dynamicity: This criterion allows distinguishing between structural and dynamic properties. Structural properties would be those that define the static qualities of the material entity, that is, those that do not involve a change of state, such as thickness, robustness, or mass. Dynamic properties, on the other hand, are those that would allow the system to experience change, or to go from one state to another. These include, for instance, enthalpy or polarity in chemical and biochemical systems, respectively. 

Two important notes should be made regarding the static/dynamic distinction. First, structural properties can be part of scientific laws as much as dynamic properties do, for they also relate to other properties in a patterned way. Second, the distinction between structural and dynamic properties in living entities is more methodological than ontological, since it is a well-known fact that structural properties of organisms are normally maintained through complex feedback loops enacted by—or permitted by, properly speaking—dynamic properties. These properties might be called “homeostatic”, constituting those that enact changes of state in the components of a system to allow the system as a whole to remain in a given macro-state. 

-Related to their quantitative nature: Properties can lastly be separated based on whether they are qualitative or quantitative. Among qualitative properties, the most important one is, for sure, existence. Quantitative properties, on the contrary, are those that show some kind of numerosity or degree, and therefore can be described using mathematical tools. Regarding this distinction, it is important to notice that most—if not all—qualitative properties are sustained by underlying quantitative properties and processes, and that properties that might be considered qualitative at first turn out to be quantitative when inspected more closely. For instance, color, a candidate qualitative property, is sustained by a complex network of quantitative events, such as the reflectance of electromagnetic waves and the firings of neurons in the retina and brain of the percipient organism. Even existence, in the case of organisms, is brought about by many underlying quantitative properties of organisms, such as the capacity for metabolism. This has a methodological consequence: The advancement of science consists, to a good extent, of finding the quantitative properties and processes that underlie qualitative phenomena. 

Conclusion

Properties are a fundamental piece in any metaphysics and in any scientific work, yet I haven’t previously reflected on them thoroughly. From the point of view of a naturalist ontology—a metaphysics compatible with a scientific worldview, the one that I favor—properties can be defined as the features that characterize a material entity. This simple definition implies several ontological and epistemological theses, such as, for instance, the reliance of properties on things or entities (i.e., the fact that there are no properties in themselves), or that describing an object boils down to enumerating its properties. 

There are different kinds of properties, depending on the criteria used to classify them. I have distinguished between essential and subordinate properties, actual and potential properties, primary and secondary properties, structural and dynamic properties, and quantitative and qualitative properties. There is no single way of classifying them, and numerous overlaps exist between these different classes. However, all of them capture objective features of properties that allow making sense of their bewildering diversity.
