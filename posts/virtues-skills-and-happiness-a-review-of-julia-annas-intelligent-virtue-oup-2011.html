Virtues, Skills, and Happiness: A Review of Julia Annas' "Intelligent Virtue" (OUP, 2011)

In my opinion, the concept of virtue is one of the most interesting ideas in both positive psychology, practical philosophy, and ethical theory. Although discussions of virtue were common in Ancient Greece, the standard account posits that the notion of virtue disappeared from ethical discourse with the arrival of Modernity until it was rescued from the dustbin of history by Alasdair MacIntyre and other neo-Aristotelian philosophers. I’m not fully convinced of this story, because traces of virtue ethics can be found in thinkers as modern as Hume or Kant. Be that as it may, nowadays talk of virtue is quite common not only in moral philosophy but also in psychology or even philosophy of science. Among other reasons, I think virtue is important because it links character traits to ethical behavior, providing an account as to what makes the difference between doing the right thing by chance and doing it consistently. 

However, the concept of virtue poses a series of philosophical (as well as scientific) challenges that every consistent and thoroughly virtue-based approach to ethics must address. And this is what Julia Annas’ Intelligent Virtue (Oxford University Press, 2011), my second reading this summer, aims to achieve. Julia Annas is a well-known scholar both in the areas of classical Greek philosophy and ethics. She is the author of The Morality of Happiness –a highly influential book in modern moral philosophy–, in which she explores the common threats uniting all classical Greek ethical theories, namely the concepts of eudaimonia and areté (virtue). And somehow, these concepts are also the focus of Intelligent Virtue, in which she aims to present a robust account of virtue as a central concept in ethical theory. This account is based on two key ideas, that the author readily presents in the Introduction of the book: (1) that “exercising a virtue [can be compared to] exercising a practical skill” (p. 1); and (2) “that it is plausible to see virtue as actually constituting (wholly or in part) (…) happiness” or flourishing (p. 1). In that sense, the resonance with Aristotle's virtue theory is evident, and, in fact, after reading a few pages of the book, I quickly took it as a sophisticated and updated version of this theory (which for me is a good thing, since I love Aristotle’s virtue ethics). 

However, it can be argued that in Intelligent Virtue, Annas advances many more interesting ideas than the two already mentioned. In my opinion, a compelling way of approaching the book is to see it a series of questions pertinent to a theory of virtue that Annas addresses step by step. These are the following:

1.- What is a virtue from a psychological point of view?

2.- How virtues are acquired? How do they work?

3.- What is the scope of virtue?

4.- What is the phenomenology of virtue? How does it feel to act virtuously?

5.- Are there many virtues or just one overarching virtue?

6.- What is the relationship between virtue and goodness?

7.- Is virtue enough to lead a happy life?

All except one of these questions are tackled in a separate chapter, with the latest being addressed in the last two chapters of the book. Let us briefly review the key points of Annas' response.

So, for a start, what is a virtue from a psychological point of view? Is it a stable condition, like a character trait, or a more volatile process, like an emotion? Annas opts for the former option: “A virtue is a lasting feature of a person, a tendency for the person to be in a certain way” (p. 8). Now, this does not mean that virtue is something that an individual can have in an unchanging and lasting way without doing anything on her part; in this sense, a virtue is an active, developing feature that evolves whenever its possessor acts in a virtuous way. Besides, Annas convincingly argues that virtues are characterized by some other properties, like reliability (making the behavior of a virtuous person predictable) or deepness (constituting a central feature of a personand not an accidental one). In this sense, we would be apt to say that, for Annas, virtue is kind of like a personality trait. (Maybe the only difference between virtues and personality traits is, though, that virtues constitute ethically desirable personality traits, while that is not the case with other personality traits, like conscientiousness or neuroticism.)

If virtues are deep and reliable, yet dynamic character traits, two questions quickly pop out into our minds, namely: How virtues are acquired? And how do they work? These questions are the subject matter of Chapter 3, entitled “Skilled and virtuous action”. And with such a title, Annas is already pointing to the answer: virtues are acquired (and work) like practical skills. The virtue-as-practical-skill analogy constitutes a central theme in Intelligent Virtue, and therefore Annas devotes a significant number of pages to its discussion. 

Annas successfully shows the differences between a practical skill and a mere habit; the main difference lies in cognitive effort: when performing habits, individuals act routinely, while practical skills are more “cognitively demanding”, so to say. By that, I mean that, according to Annas, practical skills require an understanding of what one is doing as well as the capacity to provide reasons for how a certain practical skill is exercised the way it is. That is definitely not the case with habits. Annas calls this need to understand what one is doing typical of practical skills (and virtues) the “drive to aspire”. The drive to aspire is important since it allows Annas, among other things, to respond to some objections raised against a virtue-centered approach to ethics, such as that of the scope of virtue. And that is the topic of Chapter 4 of Intelligent Virtue.  

What is the scope of virtue? By that, we are asking whether a virtuous person can somehow transcend the context in which she lives, acting in a way that, although virtuous, is contrary to what she has learned from the members of her community. In other words: can a person who has been taught to be virtuous by her parents and teachers reveal against them in the name of virtue?

To have a better grasp of this problem, it is important to stress one aspect of Annas' account of virtue, namely the role granted to the process of learning a virtue. Virtues are always learned in a social context; first, apprentices of virtue imitate their role models until they are mature enough to act for themselves based on their own understanding of the situation. But if virtue is learned from others and is completely embedded in its social context, does that mean that virtuous action is relative or that it cannot transcend the society in which it is expressed? That is the impression that we got when we look at, for instance, ancient Stoics, such as Epictetus, Seneca, or Marcus Aurelius. Marcus Aurelius, for instance, is frequently considered a quintessential example of a virtuous person; yet, he had no trouble with slavery.  So, even if we come to develop as virtuous persons, are we still trapped in our social context?

To that question, Annas replies that, in a way, yes. Given that we learn to be virtuous from the members of our community, it is difficult for us to transcend it. But not impossible. As I recall, Annas points to the drive to aspire as a mechanism by which we can somehow “escape” our social context and achieve a more universal or context-independent way of thinking about right and wrong. As we develop as virtuous persons, our capacity to reason about virtuous action is enlarged, and we can use our critical capacities to eventually reach a conclusion that goes against our community. 

Annas argues that, when we become virtuous persons, we come to be members of the universal community of virtuous people, which goes beyond a particular space and time. In belonging to such a community, we can seek inspiration from others in different contexts to conclude what to do in our particular circumstances. And that can include, of course, fighting to change the social context in which we were raised and to which we owe our own development of virtue. But ultimately, there is no guarantee that we would reach such a stage. In this sense, we have to accept that, if not determined, virtue is certainly constrained by the context in which it develops. 

The next question that Annas addresses in Intelligent Virtue have to do with the phenomenology of virtue: Howdoes it feel to be virtuous? Or, more precisely, how does it feel to act virtuously? Annas responds to such questions by drawing on positive psychology, which I find particularly stimulating. Simply put, acting virtuously feels like being in a state of flow. Flow is a concept developed by psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi (pronounced “cheeks sent me high”) that refers to the mental state we are in when the activities we are doing are interesting to us and match our abilities (that is, they are neither too difficult nor too easy for us). Flow is characterized by deep concentration and engagement, such that we lose the perception of time and ourselves. This makes acting virtuously enjoyable. Describing the phenomenology of virtue as one of flow further strengthens the practical skill analogy, given that practical skills, unlike habits, involve flow feelings when they are being exercised. As I said, this account of the phenomenology of virtue is deeply interesting and appealing. However, from a scientific point of view, it ultimately constitutes a scientific hypothesis to be tested with the tools of psychological science, as Annas herself acknowledges in the Conclusion of the book.

So, at this stage we have a relatively well-articulated view of virtue: we know what it is, how it develops, what is its scope, and what it is like to act virtuously. So far, we have been assuming that there are separate (and relatively autonomous, we might add) virtues, such as courage, generosity, or magnanimity. However, if we stick to the virtue ethics tradition, this is far from obvious: philosophers since antiquity have been wondering whether there are many different virtues or, conversely, whether all virtuous action is just a manifestation of a single, overarching virtue. Annas devotes Chapter 6 to advancing her views on this topic, which, as far as I can tell, is highly influenced by Aristotle. In a nutshell, Annas suggests that even though virtues can be divided into different categories, they are linked by what Aristotle called phronesis or practical wisdom. In Annas’ words: “Virtues which are distinct when we start to learn them turn out to be related because they all involve the same practical intelligence, which operates over life as a whole, not in different ways in separate compartments of life.” (p. 99) As Annas points out, in a way this weakens the virtue-as-practical-skill analogy since, whereas practical skills are local and do not imply each other, virtues are ultimately unified inasmuch as they are driven by practical intelligence. 

So now we are left with two remaining questions: What is the relationship between virtue and goodness? And is virtue enough for leading a happy life? Regarding the former question, Annas’ response is straightforward: Virtue reveals a commitment to goodness or positive value. Acting virtuously necessarily implies that we honestly care about good things and want to bring them about. This points to a fundamental asymmetry between virtues and vices: Virtuous people aim at achieving or maintaining something good, while people that act in a vicious way (for instance, being a coward) do not aim at anything in particular, but are only failing at aiming for something good. The commitment to goodness also allows us to distinguish between virtues and neutral traits since the latter do not imply a commitment to any moral value. In this sense, Annas argues that, in addition to practical intelligence, commitment to goodness is a unifying factor for all virtues since it allows us to group all of them in a separate category from other kinds of traits. 

And, finally, the key question: Is virtue enough for living a happy life? As said before, this question is addressed in two chapters (8 and 9) of Intelligent Virtue. In Chapter 8, Annas offers a critique of most contemporary conceptions of happiness, including those of happiness as pleasure, getting what you want, and feeling satisfied with your life. As an alternative, Annas supports eudaimonism, in which happiness is seen as the overall aim of our lives, an aim whose specific content may vary as we develop as rational agents. But what is the role of virtue in such a life? Annas' response is the following: “Whether I live happily will depend at least in part on the dispositions I have developed as I have lived my life, the way these are maintained or decay, and their mutual interrelations.” (p. 151) For Annas, happiness, as well as virtue, is dynamic in the sense that our understanding of it changes as we go through our lives. In the same way we learn to become virtuous, we also learn to be happy as our skills as rational agents become sharper and we come to understand better what we want out of life. In this sense, in the eudaimonist tradition, there is no static and a priori conception of happiness to which we should stick, but only an ongoing reflection about happiness that takes place as we live our lives and become more skillful at the art of living and becoming virtuous.  

These are the ideas advanced by Annas in Intelligent Virtue, which can constitute an interesting reading in parallel to more scientific, or empirical approaches to virtue, such as those of positive psychologists.
