On the Voluntary or Involuntary Nature of Beliefs

There is this classic topic in epistemology or the philosophy of knowledge about to what extent I, as an individual, can choose my beliefs: is there room for choosing the things that I believe, or, on the contrary, are my beliefs beyond my voluntary control? Here I will present my sketch of a response to that question (I apologize for not taking into account the relevant philosophical literature in my exposition). To provide such an answer, let’s begin by clarifying what I understand as “a belief”. 

Definition: a belief is a proposition, or a set of interrelated propositions on whichever topic (ethics, epistemology, natural facts, aesthetics, daily life, and so on) that a certain person holds, and to which she shows a certain degree of confidence. 

In other words, beliefs are propositions towards which we maintain a propositional attitude of assent. That excludes from the definition all those propositions that we momentarily happen to think about but towards which we don’t show too much conviction, or all those hypotheses that we adopt for the sake of conducting our investigations, but that for the moment do not inspire us too much trust. Importantly, for a given person a proposition can constitute a belief even if the grounds for having confidence in it are ill-founded; that is, even if such belief does not constitute “knowledge” according to the classic definition. The only requisite for being a belief is that the person who holds it really “believes” in its truthfulness or validity.

Now, I think that the above definition necessarily leads us to a certain conclusion on whether we’re free to choose our beliefs: no, we haven’t got such freedom. Let me articulate the argument for this step by step:

1.- Beliefs depend on assent.

2.- Assent, on its part, hinges upon a set of normative standards which, ultimately, are what guide us in determining whether a certain belief is worth assenting. 

3.- These normative standards, even if flawed, are binding: once we have them, they force us to assent to those beliefs that satisfy them and to reject those that don’t. 

4.- For all the above, I think that we’re sort of “forced” to believe what we believe if only for the simple reason that what we believe has passed the criteria established by our normative standards for assent.

Let me illustrate this chain of reasoning with an example: regarding factual beliefs, a normative standard that guides the assent of propositions in most people is correspondence with reality. In matters of fact, we only believe what we consider to be true, or an accurate representation of what is out there. This happens even if our “correspondence standard” is rather undemanding (that is, if we take poor or flawed data as confirmatory evidence for a given statement about the state of the world). If a certain proposition crosses the threshold of empirical adequacy, then we cannot but assent to such proposition, and therefore, turn it into one of our beliefs. So, regarding factual matters, we cannot help but believe what we think is true and reject what we think is factually false. In certain cases, and for some people, though, the set of normative standards that guide their assent is completely arbitrary from an epistemological point of view. These can include, for instance, the famous fallacy of argumentum ad verecundiam, or the assent of a proposition only because it is believed by someone that a certain person deems as an authority. But be that as it may, once a certain proposition satisfies a normative standard, we’re forced to assent to it and start to believe in it. Whether we like it or not.

So, I think that we cannot choose the beliefs that we happen to have. That said, this doesn’t mean that we cannot do anything at all to change what we believe, even if indirectly. I think there are at least two ways in which we can contribute to that change, and about which we can exert a certain extent of voluntary control: 

a) To expose ourselves to new information that can alter our evaluation of a certain claim or proposition: we can, willingly, get ourselves exposed to certain pieces of evidence that may contribute to the changing of our beliefs. These pieces of evidence might convince us that, according to our actual standards for assent, our previous belief was not justified enough, so that we should change it. For instance, if I’m a progressive, I can start to read conservative newspapers to see if the approach that they follow makes me reevaluate at least part of my political beliefs. Similarly, if I believe that theory A is correct, I might willingly start to read supporters of theory B to see if my assent to theory A is warranted. All these actions can eventually lead to a change in (at least part of) my beliefs, and they are to a certain extent under my control. 

b) To reflect on our normative standards for assent: we can voluntarily assess the epistemic quality of our normative standards, such that we get rid of those that are ill-founded and substitute them for sounder standards. If so, ultimately that can make us change part of our beliefs as well. For instance, if, for whatever reason, I happen to believe something because someone I consider an authority has said so, and for no other reasons, I could get rid of this belief by questioning my (probably tacit) normative standard of argumentum ad verecundiam. It is highly likely that if I abandon the argumentum ad verecundiam as a normative standard, then I will end up changing all those beliefs that depended on it. And again, the reevaluation of my normative standards is relatively under my control. 

In sum, even though we cannot have direct control over what we happen to believe, because the normative standards that determine our beliefs are binding to us, I can perform certain types of behavior that, ultimately, make me reevaluate my beliefs and, most likely, change some of them. 

Addenda: the will to believe

The conclusion stated above, though sound, leaves me thinking about the so-called “will to believe”. If what I have argued is true, then what is it of one of William James’ most famous ideas? Are we allowed to voluntarily choose what we believe? Is this even possible? I don’t have a firm and definite position (one might say “a belief”) on this issue, but to my understanding, there could be two different ways in which the will to believe might be understood if interpreted under the framework presented in the previous paragraphs: 

a) The will to believe might constitute a direct violation of at least one of our normative standards: that could happen if, for instance, I start to willingly believe something even if it has not reached the threshold established by my normative standards. For instance, if I start to believe that the sky is red despite the overwhelming evidence on the contrary. I would say that this is most likely impossible at a psychological level because it would lead to such a degree of inauthenticity and cognitive dissonance that most human beings simply cannot bear. As stated before, I think our normative standards are binding; this means that their violation might lead to a psychological contradiction so big that it’s difficult to handle. 

b) The will to believe might reflect our espousing of “utility” as a normative standard: maybe what James was tacitly assuming was that all those who willingly espouse an idea even if there are no real objective grounds for it are doing so because they hold “utility” as their chief normative standard, standing over others such as empirical adequacy or logical soundness. Under this scenario, the will to believe might be a more sensible stance or doctrine. I believe, however, that for most people it would be unpalatable if the higher standing of utility as a normative standard implies almost completely ignoring the rest of the standards, such that we believe something because we deem it as useful even if it’s completely false from an empirical point of view. 

So, if the framework presented here is correct, then only a bounded kind of will to believe might be reasonable, if any.
