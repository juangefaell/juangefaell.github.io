Is Positive Psychology Value-Laden? Yes, But No Problem: A Review of Prinzing (2020)

A classic topic in the philosophy of science is the relationship between science and values: is science value-free or value-laden? And if the latter, which values affect science and how? The received view in philosophy of science is that science is value-free. This view was based on the fact/value dichotomy, according to which there is a neat distinction between descriptive and normative statements. Scientific statements would be purely descriptive, while normative propositions would occur only in ethics and moral philosophy. 


The history of the fact/value dichotomy is complex, but to our purposes it suffices to say that most philosophers of science today agree that science is value-laden (see, for instance, Allchin 1999). Modern philosophical analyses of science have revealed the many ways in which non-epistemic values influence the scientific enterprise, from theory justification to data presentation.

However, despite the agreement of most philosophers of science, many scientists still believe that theirs is a value-free activity. And positive psychologists –those who study psychological wellbeing– are no exception. That is why I find one of the papers that I read last weekend especially interesting. The main thesis of this paper, written by philosopher Michael Prinzing and entitled “Positive psychology is value-laden –It’s time to embrace it”, is that the value-ladenness of positive psychology, far from being a bad thing, can help make this discipline a more rigorous one. According to Prinzing, the real problem is not that positive psychology is value-laden, but that these values are rarely openly discussed. In the following I’m going to briefly present the arguments in Prinzing’s paper, although in a personal and unsystematic way. 

Prinzing’s paper is divided in three main sections: first, he tries to show why positive psychology is value-laden; second, he responds to some objections raised against such diagnosis; and third, he shows which benefits derive from acknowledging and embracing the value-ladenness of positive psychology. Let’s briefly comment each section.

Why Positive Psychology is Value-Laden

There are many reasons for the value-ladenness of positive psychology. However, for me the most convincing one is put forward by Prinzing in the only footnote of his paper (it was rather surprising for me that he does not discuss it in the main text). Put simply, this argument states that positive psychology, as well medicine and many social sciences, are value-laden because they deal with what are called “thick concepts”. Thick concepts are those that have both empirical and normative aspects. 

Take, for instance, the concept of “courage”, the example put forward by Prinzing. Courage is not only a person’s willingness to expose himself to a dangerous situation, but to do it for a good cause. For instance, we do not usually consider courageous a person who walks off a cliff for no apparent reason. In order for her to be considered courageous, we must check that she is doing so in order to achieve something valuable. Now, the point is that determining what is good or valuable is a moral exercise. Given that positive psychologists study such things as courage –that is, thick concepts– they cannot avoid moral reasoning. Otherwise, they would not be able to distinguish which behaviors can count as acts of courage and which ones cannot. Investigating wellbeing, moral excellence or flourishing requires engaging in moral evaluation.

A Possible Objection

Once Prinzing shows why positive psychology is a value-laden discipline, he discusses two possible objections. Here I’m going to focus only on one of these, namely, that positive psychologists do not make value judgements, but only study what ordinary people think is valuable. This has been defended by Kenneth Sheldon and Ed Diener[1], two leading figures in the hedonist-subjectivist conception of psychological wellbeing. According to Prinzing, these authors argue that, in an increasingly democratic world, experts should not determine what constitutes a good life, but only to register what most people counts as a good life. Thus, psychological theories of wellbeing must be content-free. 

After criticizing the Satisfaction With Life Scale (SWL), the main instrument Diener and colleagues have devised to measure subjective wellbeing, Prinzing argues that the very fact of letting people freely decide what is wellbeing, without any further consideration, represents itself a very strong value commitment, a commitment in tune with political liberalism. So it is simply not the case that positive psychologists are completely value-free, even when they pretend to. 

A Value-Laden Science of Wellbeing

So, positive psychology is definitely value-laden. We have two options, then: either we accept it, or we give up the task of creating a science of wellbeing. Given that the latter scenario is highly undesirable –since wellbeing is a very important topic for our lives, and science is our best way to know empirical reality–, we are almost obliged to choose the first option. But if we do so, what a thoroughly and consciously value-laden psychology positive would require of positive psychologists? According to Prinzing, it requires three things: 

  1. Pretensions of valuative neutrality, currently commonplace in positive psychology, should be abandoned. 
  2. Values underlying different theories advanced by positive psychologists must always made explicit in their scientific papers. 
  3. Validation of psychological constructs must be done also at the valuative level, and not only at the empirical level. In other words, justification of psychological constructs must involve a discussion as to why a given construct is preferable for measuring wellbeing than its alternatives from a normative point of view. 

If these commitments are taken seriously, then Prinzing predicts two positive outcomes: first, a better theorizing about the good life, and second –and perhaps paradoxically– a more objective positive psychology. 

As for the first outcome, Prinzing argues that an open acknowledgment of value-ladenness would oblige positive psychologists to theoretically justify why a given set of values is more adequate or relevant to account for the good life. This, in turn, would require that positive psychologists become familiar with the corresponding philosophical literature. Prinzing argues that, just as philosophers are required to be up to date with science when making empirical claims, scientists must be up to date with the relevant philosophical literature if they are to make evaluative or normative claims.

As for the second point, Prinzing argues that we should give up the notion of objectivity as valuative neutrality. For disciplines that deal with thick concepts, such as medicine or positive psychology, objectivity should be understood as transparency about ethical and theoretical commitments: “it seems to me that the ideal of scientific objectivity [at least for positive psychology] ought to be one of openness and intellectual humility. That is, all claims –be they empirical, mathematical, evaluative, etc.– should be made clearly and candidly so that they can be subjected to critical reflection and ongoing, public critique and debate.” (Prinzing 2020:7). 

Just as we require scientists to be transparent about the statistical methods employed (and, ultimately, about the raw data obtained), and consider that those who do not provide enough information about them are not doing good science, the same ought to happen with value commitments. Transparency allows for honest discussion, and this, according to Prinzing, makes it easier to get closer to the truth, even if supporters of different theories have very different biases or preferences.

Conclusion

In short, Prinzing clearly shows that positive psychology is value-laden –that non-epistemic values ​​are unavoidable– and that, if this embraced by positive psychologists, then the whole field of positive psychology would probably become more objective. I completely agree with Prinzing, and I think his is a superb paper from the philosophical point of view. However, I think that it leaves at least three important issues open: 

a) Prinzing's value-laden thesis calls for further articulation of the concept of objectivity as transparency, which is only very superficially outlined in his paper. 

b) If Prinzing's thesis about the need for value transparency is taken seriously, then consensus in psychological science may be even more difficult to attain, given that different scientist would likely espouse different sets of incommensurable values. If consensus is seen as a valuable feature of science, then how to reconcile value discrepancy with scientific consensus becomes a pressing problem.

c) If thick concepts do really exist, then what kind of world is this in which values ​​and facts are sometimes intertwined? The received view assumes a relatively simple ontology: there are objective facts and subjective value judgements, completely separated. Which ontology may provide an alternative to the received view? Although I do not have the answer to such question, I think that the different varieties of moral naturalism (including Philippa Foot’s) are probably the best alternatives yet devised. But they have to be further developed before constituting a convincing alternative
