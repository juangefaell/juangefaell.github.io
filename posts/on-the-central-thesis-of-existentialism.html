On the Central Thesis of Existentialism

In Existentialism and Humanism, Jean-Paul Sartre aims to offer a positive and constructive picture of the doctrine of existentialism. At the time at which the conference on which this short book is based was uttered, shortly after the end of WWII, Europe was eager to find a philosophy that could help people to recover from the barbarism of the Nazis and to face the future with renewed hope. Existentialism was one of the candidate philosophies to accomplish those tasks —being a popular doctrine at that time—, but many still saw it as a rather bleak and shady philosophy. Sartre set himself the task of dispelling this view of existentialism and portraying it in a way that could appeal to his contemporaries. In addition to that, and as Arlette Elkem-Sartre (Jean-Paul’s adopted daughter) explains in the preface of the book, with the conference that inspired Existentialism and Humanism, Sartre also wanted to respond to some of his colleagues who, from a Marxist perspective, criticized existentialism as being contrary to this political doctrine. 

In my opinion, and despite its historical interest, Existentialism and Humanism fails to accomplish the twofold task that Sartre set out to do with it. In a nutshell, I think it fails because the main idea on which it’s based is ill-founded, so all that comes from it must be rejected. The central thesis of existentialism is, according to Sartre, that existence precedes essence. From this thesis, all the rest of the existentialist views follow. 

I think that the central thesis of existentialism is at the same time conceptually fallacious, empirically false, and potentially politically regressive. But before explaining why, let me enunciate it in a little more detail. Sartre’s reasoning could be stated as follows:

Premise 1: Whatever essence humans might have must come from God. 

Premise 2: God doesn’t exist. 

From both premises, Sartre derives the following conclusion:

Conclusion: Given that God doesn’t exist, humans don’t have any a priori essence. 

Yet people can create their own essence as they live.

Therefore, in humans existence precedes essence. That is, we start to exist, and then we acquire an essence, if any. With this thesis, Sartre wanted to stress the role of our free actions in determining what we are and what we eventually come to be. 

I will ignore premise 2 and assume, with Sartre, that belief in God is unwarranted (this is a view that, indeed, I lean toward). My problem with the central thesis of existentialism lies then in its first premise, namely, that any essence whatsoever that humans might have must come from God, and the conclusion it follows from it once it is coupled with premise 2 (i.e., that given that God does not exist, then there is no human essence). As stated above, I believe this premise and its ensuing conclusion are conceptually fallacious, empirically false, and potentially politically regressive. In the remainder of this post, I will try to explain why I believe this is so. 

Conceptually fallacious

I believe the central thesis of existentialism is conceptually fallacious because it rests on a false dichotomy: either essence precedes existence, and our essence is granted to us by God (as religious believers think), or existence precedes essence —and therefore, there is no human nature because God doesn’t exist (as Sartre believes). I don’t see why we must be forced to choose between these two unsatisfactory alternatives. I see at least two more options that seem more plausible to me, and that Sartre completely disregards in his presentation, except for but a few comments on French radical liberals: 

-Essence is immanent to existence: Instead of essence preceding existence or the other way around, the two might coexist and co-determine one another. If so, no abstract essence would exist before an individual comes into being, but neither such individual would be completely free to self-determine, but she will be constrained by her immanent essence. 

-Essence is an “ontogenetically a priori”, yet a “phylogenetically a posteriori”: Many might be skeptical towards a strong version of the thesis that essence precedes existence (the opposite of the central thesis of existentialism), mainly because it sounds too similar to the —nowadays rejected by most— Plato’s doctrine of ideas. Still, skeptics might be open to a softer version of it. One soft version of this thesis is that essence might be an ontogenetically a priori but a phylogenetically a posteriori. In other words: essence might precede any specific individual developmentally (that is, her essence might be determined by her genes even before she was born), but still this essence be the product of a long evolutionary history (and therefore a “phylogenetically a posteriori”). If so, each of us as individuals would constitute a specific iteration of an essence set in a collection of genes whose evolution has been the result of evolution by natural selection.

I don’t know whether any of these options is actually correct, or whether there might be other alternatives to the two options posed by Sartre. Anyway, my point here is that it is simply false to present the debate in a dichotomous manner as if there were no third ways.  

Empirically false

In addition to being formulated in a conceptually fallacious way, I believe that the central thesis of existentialism is also most likely empirically false, as it goes against findings in both biology and psychology. I think that throughout their history, these two disciplines have gathered enough knowledge to strongly suggest that there is indeed a human nature or essence, even if this knowledge only allows us to define such nature in a vague, yet thick, way (to use Nussbaum’s expression).

As for biology, it would be odd to assume, as Sartre appears to do, that all animal species have a nature, but humans don’t. That would be a biologically untenable form of human exceptionalism. It’s true that some authors have argued that evolutionary theory makes the idea of a species’ nature or essence meaningless, because the populations that make up a species at a particular time evolve, which means that the properties that define them change. However, I don’t find that argument particularly compelling, as evolutionary change is a very slow process and, at a given time t it’s always possible to identify a series of essential traits (i.e., a nature) that would help identify an individual as a member of a given species. In that sense, an essence would be, to put it with Boyd, a temporally bounded homeostatic property cluster. So, returning to our previous point, if we don’t want to commit to an extreme form of human exceptionalism, then we should either reject the idea that all animal species lack a nature or reject Sartre’s claim that there is not such a thing as human nature and that it’s up to us to create our own nature. And if one considers coherence with the best scientific knowledge a worthy epistemic value when doing philosophy, then one should be inclined to choose the latter alternative over the former.

As for psychology, many authors have put forward suggestive evidence of a series of traits that might be specific to humans as a species, and that therefore constitute potential candidates for key features of our nature. These go from the more classic suggestions, such as symbolic and articulate language, to more modern proposals such as shared intentionality. Other sub-disciplines in psychology have found common patterns of thought and behavior in humans from different cultures whose commonalities might be best explained as a reflection of our nature (see a candidate list reproduced at the end of Steven Pinker’s The Blank Slate).

Sartre’s thesis that there is no such thing as human nature (or essence) is so untenable from an empirical point of view that in Existentialism and Humanism, he feels obliged to put forward a watered-down version of it to avoid some obvious objections to his view. This watered-down version of human nature is what he calls the “human condition”. The human condition would comprise all those situations that most human beings experience in their lives, and that spark very similar emotional reactions in all of us. With the human condition, Sartre seeks to avoid the criticism that his thesis cannot account for all those experiences that are shared by human beings. 

The problem with the idea of a human condition is that it would be a miracle that such a condition exists at all if there was not a human nature. To put it differently: It would be a miracle that a certain kind of situation would spark the same emotion in a series of unrelated human beings if they didn’t share the same cognitive and affective mechanisms. An obvious example: Because humans have to eat to sustain themselves biologically, and because the need for autonomy is a basic psychological need (both traits being part of human nature), we can make sense of the negative feelings a person might experience if she cannot find a proper job that allows her to autonomously get the food she needs. In sum, a basic notion of human nature is necessary to make sense of the human condition. 

Politically regressive

I have sketched the arguments for why I believe the central thesis of existentialism is both conceptually fallacious and, most likely, empirically false. But I also believe, as stated some paragraphs above, that it’s also potentially politically regressive. I see two reasons for why this is so. 

First, the extreme emphasis Sartre places on individual freedom and self-determination might resonate more with a right-wing worldview than with a progressive one. To my mind, at least part of the reason why progressives support generous welfare measures and good working conditions is that we believe that how well individuals fare in life depends largely on morally arbitrary factors that go beyond an individual’s control, such as their family background or the education they get as children. If we believe that individuals are fully responsible for their situation (and not their parent’s financial status, access to proper education, and so on), then we would be less prone to support a universal welfare state that would prevent anyone from falling into poverty. 

Second, I think that the most well-argued defenses of a left-wing approach to politics assume the existence of a series of basic human needs that any well-ordered society must help to satisfy. These needs, in turn, rely on the existence of a more or less fixed human nature. Among these proposals, the capabilities approach of Nussbaum and Sen stands out, in my opinion. In an Aristotelian vein, the capabilities approach posits that there are a series of human potentials that, provided good conditions for their development are granted, all human beings enjoy realizing and fulfilling. These conditions needed for the development of human potentials would be established by our nature, and without the latter, it would be quite hard to justify, for instance, the right to public access to healthcare or education. The denial of human nature fits best with a neoliberal approach to politics, in which all our needs, which are unique to each of us as individuals, would be best satisfied by the market.

Conclusion

I acknowledge that existentialism might be an empowering philosophy because of the emphasis it places on self-determination. That might be the reason why many respectable thinkers, including Michael Ruse and David Barash among those with a biological background, are attracted to existentialism. But I wonder whether it is strictly necessary to ground a defense of human freedom on such an erroneous idea as the central thesis of existentialism. I don’t see autonomy as opposed to human nature. The way I see it, human nature gives room for a certain degree of free will –a “free will worth wanting”, as Daniel Dennett has put it– that should be defended and encouraged. Besides that, existentialism might be also criticized for its bad arguments in favor of the view that there is no meaning to life, which I have discussed in an earlier post. For all these reasons, my final verdict on existentialism is that it is not a very convincing philosophy and therefore, that it should be rejected.
