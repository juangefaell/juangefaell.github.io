Rationality and Theory Choice: Some Reflections on Kuhn (1977)

Although I have recently co-authored a paper on Kuhn, I do not consider myself a Kuhnian. By this I mean that, even though I find some of the ideas put forward by Kuhn very useful and interesting (particularly that of incommensurability), I do not agree with the entire Kuhnian view of science. Hilary Putnam (1997) once said that one does not have to be a Wittgensteinian to learn from Wittgenstein, or a Kantian to learn from Kant, and that’s exactly how I feel about Kuhn (and the rest of prominent twentieth century philosophers of science, by the way). 

I have just said that I find the Kuhnian notion of incommensurability –properly revised, I would add– quite interesting; another Kuhnian insight that I find stimulating is his description of the role that epistemic values play in science. In his paper “Objectivity, value judgement and theory choice” (1977), Kuhn stressed the importance of epistemic values in theory evaluation by scientists. In this post I will sketch Kuhn’s observations on this topic, and highlight the problem that it poses for those of us who are friends of rationality in science.

How Rational is Theory Choice?

In The Structure of Scientific Revolutions, Kuhn suggested that there are no purely objective criteria that scientists can use to end paradigm-changing revolutions. Given that competing paradigms are incommensurable –that is, that there are no neutral grounds from which to compare those paradigms–, theory choice and evaluation necessarily involve subjective factors. According to Kuhn, it is simply not possible to deem one paradigm superior to another on the basis of a single criterion, as logical positivists or falsationists used to argue. Quite the contrary, there are several non-strictly objective factors that play a role in the choice of a scientific community to switch from one paradigm to another.

The Kuhnian thesis that there is no purely objective criterion by which to decide between two competing paradigms led some philosophers at that time to think that Kuhn was arguing that theory choice is a completely irrational or arbitrary process. But that is not what Kuhn was arguing for; in my opinion, the best way to understand his position is that theory evaluation and choice is neither irrational nor algorithmic.  

The Role of Epistemic Values

To see why theory evaluation and choice are not irrational, we should take a look precisely at Kuhn’s discussion of epistemic values. The main point of his 1977 paper is that, at least since the Scientific Revolution, all scientists tend to share a series of epistemic values that allow for theory evaluation. Such epistemic values, although not totally objective, provide at least a rational guide for distinguishing between competing theories or paradigms. These values are accuracy, consistence, scope, simplicity and fruitfulness. 

Accuracy is the degree of agreement between experimental results and the predictions of a theory. Consistency refers to the degree of internal coherence of a theory, as well as to its compatibility with other relevant scientific theories. Scope amounts to the degree of applicability of the theory beyond the initial range of phenomena for which it was originally conceived. Simplicity is the ability of a theory to explain a given set of phenomena by appeal to one or a few explanatory principles. Finally, fruitfulness is equal to the heuristic power of a theory, or the ability to stimulate new research and lead to new and unexpected discoveries. 

According to Kuhn, when judging theories, scientists typically evaluate these five attributes; this is true irrespectively of the field of research or epoch. In this sense, epistemic values seem to be universal. Furthermore, there are sound reasons to think that these epistemic values are not capricious, but quite reasonable.  

Subjective Factors and Epistemic Values

However, Kuhn readily warns us that, although these values have been a crucial part of modern science since its very inception, the way they are applied to specific controversies varies significantly between scientists of different paradigms. In this sense, far from being clearcut rules for theory choice and evaluation, they are influenced by subjective factors. This is why such processes, although rational, are not algorithmic or deterministic. 

There are at least three ways in which subjective considerations affect the application of epistemic values to a given scientific theory. First, since the meaning of concepts such as “accuracy” or “consistency” is not univocal, scientists with different backgrounds may understand them in significantly different ways, leading to differences of opinion about the merits of a given theory. For instance, one scientist would understand “consistency” as mostly having to do with internal coherence, while another scientist would argue that what consistency really amounts to is compatibility with prior knowledge. 

Second, the structure of scientific theories under evaluation is not always as explicit and unambiguous as we would like; therefore, disagreements can also arise when applying epistemic values to a specific theory because there is not always consensus on what the precise structure of such theory is. For example, two given scientists may disagree about the degree of simplicity of a theory because of the way in which each of them conceptualizes and reconstructs it.

And third, subjective considerations may cause disagreement about which epistemic values are more important, or in other words, about how to rank the different epistemic values. For instance, one scientist may regard fruitfulness as the most important epistemic value for judging a scientific theory, while another may think that consistency with established theories should precede any other value. When this happens, scientists can still disagree about which theory to choose, even if they agree on the precise meaning of the different epistemic values, as well as on the conceptual structure of the theories involved. 

Problems Ahead

In our EJPS paper, Cristian and I have argued that this third point (disagreement about the ranking of epistemic values) is what methodological incommensurability amounts to; or, in other words, that methodological incommensurability should be understood as differences in the relative ordering of accuracy, consistence, scope, simplicity and fruitfulness by scientists from different backgrounds. Indeed, we believe this is part of the reason why supporters of the MS and the EES in evolutionary biology seem to disagree so fiercely.  

If we value scientific agreement –and I think there are several reasons to do so–, methodological incommensurability poses the problem of how to reach consensus between scientists when they disagree about the values they should prioritize when evaluating scientific theories. I don’t know how to solve this problem, but the point that I wanted to make in this post is that Kuhn’s remarks about the role of values in science set a very interesting starting point for those of us who want to defend and promote a strong notion of scientific rationality.
