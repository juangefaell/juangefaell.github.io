The Canon, year 1/reading 4—Aristophanes’ Lysistrata

My newest Western Canon reading has been Aristophanes’ Lysistrata. This is one of Aristophanes’ most renowned comedies. The plot is as follows: women, led by Lysistrata, are sick and tired of men-led wars, particularly the Peloponnesian War between Athens and Sparta that is taking place at that moment. To put an end to them, a particularly wise woman, Lysistrata, allies with a Spartan woman, Lampito, and other Athenian women to initiate a sex strike. The deal is simple: women will not allow men to have sex with them until the war ends and Athens and Sparta sign a peace treaty. To further their goal, they decide to occupy the Acropolis, where the Athenian state treasury is, so that men can no longer access war funds. In the end, and after some resistance from men, who are deeply frustrated by women’s refusal to have sex, Lysistrata and her fellows win, and Sparta and Athens sign a peace deal. 

This was a fun read. I particularly liked the scene in which Myrrhine, an ally of Lysistrata, fools her husband Cinesias into believing that they will have sex. Although at first it seems that Myrrhine will betray Lysistrata and her fellows, becoming a scab to the strike because of her uncontrollable drive for sex, it soon becomes clear that she is just stringing along Cinesias. It also struck me how recurrent sex jokes are, which I wouldn’t expect in a comedy written in 411 b. C. 

Besides that, Lysistrata got me thinking: Is it a feminist comedy? Is it a vindication of an alleged woman’s wisdom against men’s impulsivity and foolishness? To answer this, I think it’s important to know what the views of the author were—after all, it’s unlikely that someone who is not a feminist would write a feminist piece. From what I read, it seems clear that Aristophanes was certainly not a feminist (although he was apparently a pacifist, having written many comedies about the absurdity of war). That Aristophanes was not a feminist is also supported by many passages in which women are depicted as voluble, incapable of governing themselves, and even whimsical. No feminist play could ever portray women as such. That said, however, the truth is that men are no less mocked than women in Lysistrata—they are represented as irrational, impulsive, and immature, worried only about their sex lives. So, I guess that the sarcasm is, after all, somewhat symmetrical.

In conclusion, Lysistrata is an interesting read—fun at times, and with an engaging plot. Of course, from the vantage point of our modern egalitarian ideals, its stereotypical depiction of women would make many feel cringy. But I guess that’s one of the points of reading classic literature—you not only get to know what the views about women were in different epochs, but also come to fully appreciate the importance of our modern—and still ongoing—fight for equality, an important part of which consists of getting rid of the many essentialist and unsupported prejudices about how men and women allegedly are.
