Assessing Evidential Support

Traditionally, philosophers of science have placed a great emphasis on theory assessment, or the evaluation of a theory’s scientific worth. Throughout the last century, numerous lists of what have come to be known as ‘epistemic values’—aimed at helping in this task—have been crafted. One of the first lists, and perhaps the most influential, is the one put forward by Thomas S. Kuhn in his famous 1977 paper Objectivity, Value Judgement, and Theory Choice, included in his compilation of essays The Essential Tension (Kuhn, 1977). 

Kuhn’s list features five crucial criteria that would allow inter-theory comparison and assessment, even between practitioners of different paradigms: accuracy, consistency, simplicity, scope, and fruitfulness. Ultimately, these epistemic values would provide insights into a theory’s scientific quality. 

Despite the wide interest in theory assessment, much less has been written about evaluating a theory's evidential support. True, confirmation has been addressed multiple times in the philosophy of science. But too often, this discussion has focused on when we can say, based on inductive logic, that a theory has been confirmed (Carnap, 1936, 1937). Although this is certainly important, it reduces evidential assessment to the quantity of evidence in support of a theory or hypothesis, bypassing other equally important features of evidence appraisal. 

Evidential assessment is an important matter in the daily activities of most scientists. The majority of new scientific theories and hypotheses satisfy theoretical epistemic values to a reasonable extent, but they are not equally supported by the evidence. For this reason, it is highly likely that, when reviewing a paper, scientists focus more on assessing evidential support than theoretical quality. This demands a comprehensive list of evidence appraisal standards.

An enlarged list of evidence assessment criteria

As I see it, there are at least three other standards beyond the quantity of evidence that scientists normally pay attention to when assessing the evidence in favor of a theory or a hypothesis: the quality of evidence, the diversity of evidence, and, at least in biology, its relative significance. Let’s briefly go through each of these. 

Quality of evidence

Most practicing scientists hold intuitions about what it means for evidence to be of higher or lower quality. However, providing a detailed characterization of the quality of the evidence is not easy since this concept has multiple dimensions. Previous efforts at gauging this include, for instance, the hierarchy of evidence in Evidence-Based Medicine, where randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are placed on top of the evidence pyramid while case-control studies sit at the bottom (Evidence-Based Medicine Group, 1992). Though this constitutes a valuable attempt, it is too focused on the methods and interests of biomedical researchers to be helpful to evolutionary biologists. For this reason, here I’m presenting six dimensions that I believe are important in assessing the quality of evidence in evolutionary biology, with a focus on evolutionary and behavioral ecology. 

The first dimension of evidence quality involves whether a given data is quantitative as opposed to ordinal. For example, measuring the abundance of a species using a SACFOR (Little, Williams, & Trowbridge, 2009) method is certainly worse than actually counting the number of individuals along an ecosystem. 

A second dimension involves whether the evidence has been collected following a predefined protocol and with a specific objective in mind as opposed to gathering it without a clear idea of what to do with it. Normally, collecting data without a concrete purpose or using them for a different objective than the original one introduces what supporters of the Open Science Movement call researcher degrees of freedom (Bateson & Martin, 2021). Although this flaw is difficult to spot in submitted papers, some hints might suggest it. For instance, the absence of a clear rationale for the experiment or study indicates the data might have been collected without a clear objective in mind.

 A third dimension refers to the quality controls performed on data, such as inter-subject and intra-subject reliability and blind procedures in behavioral biology (Bateson & Martin, 2021). If, for instance, more than one researcher is rating the peace-making interactions between two chimpanzees, independent tests should be carried out to measure the degree of agreement of their ratings. Another example: if a scientist is conducting a caging experiment to measure predation in the field, she must devise the proper set of control conditions to partition the contribution of different effects. The absence of these quality controls broadens the range of interpretations of data, thus making it less trustworthy.

A fourth dimension, in turn, refers to whether the data allows us to draw causal inferences, whether these inferences result from experiments or statistical models (Pearl, Glymour, & Jewell, 2016). For instance, if one wants to assess the impact of tail length on reproductive success in birds, it would probably be more rigorous to experimentally modify this trait and record the reproductive outcome of the different birds than to just try to correlate the length of the tail with the number of offspring. 

A fifth dimension of evidence quality involves indicators. Most scientific evidence relies on proxies for the phenomena of interest. A majority of processes that scientists study are not directly observable, from radioactive decay and gene activity to positive emotions and political participation. That’s why we normally study them indirectly, recording observable events that are somehow connected to these processes of interest. These include emitted radiation for radioactive decay, qPCR or RNA-seq for gene activity, psychometric tests such as PANAS (the Positive And Negative Affect Schedule) for positive emotions, or voter turnout for political participation. 

Indicators have been defined as observable happenings whose occurrence is a function of an underlying unobservable process, or O = f(U). But not all indicators are equally sound. Philosopher Mario Bunge has distinguished between empirical indicators and theoretical indicators (Bunge, 2006). The main difference between both would be that, unlike empirical indicators, theoretical ones connect observable and unobservable phenomena through a rigorous theory that can be independently tested. Empirical indicators, conversely, link observable events and unobservable phenomena through a less rigorous or not independently testable hypothesis. To illustrate this distinction and see how it connects to evidential quality, let’s compare two cases. 

Genetic differentiation between populations (an unobservable phenomenon) is normally studied through FST (Sætre & Ravinet, 2019). FST is defined as the ratio of genetic variance within populations to the total genetic variance, or (HT –HS)/HT. This, in turn, relies on the average heterozygosity within populations and the total heterozygosity across populations—assuming that they constitute a single panmictic population. The FSTmeasure has a close and quantitatively precise connection to other principles of population genetics (such other Wright’s F-statistic indices), thus making it a paradigmatic theoretical indicator. 

Conversely, let’s take a concept from the Wave and Crab Littorina saxatilis’ ecotypes literature, termed “boldness” (Koch et al., 2021). Boldness is defined as the latency of a crawl-out-of-shell response of a snail after a mechanical perturbation. Boldness, which shows variation between the Crab and Wave ecotypes—being higher in the latter—has been connected to important adaptive outcomes. Specifically, “bold” individuals would survive better in wave-exposed environments because they would readily hide in crevices, while “shy” individuals (such as those belonging to the Crab ecotype) would gain a fitness advantage in crab-dominated environments because retraction to the shell makes it harder for crabs to reach the soft body parts. Thus, boldness is an indicator of a behavioral adaptation to different environments. 

While the “boldness” concept makes perfect sense, and its study might yield suggesting insights into evolution in Littorina, it most certainly constitutes an empirical indicator. As far as I’m aware, there is no mathematically precise theory connecting this allegedly behavioral response to fitness outcomes, thus being more of an educated guess than a theoretically sound proxy. 

A last criterion, well known to experimentally oriented biologists, is ecological validity. Ecological validity refers to the degree to which experimental findings in the lab or a mesocosm experiment are generalizable to the real world. One could get a hint of the importance of this dimension when field and lab studies yield contradicting results, as happens sometimes in primatology (Tomasello & Call, 2011). 

In sum, I have described six components of evidential quality: the quantitative nature of the data, its targeted and protocolized nature, the presence of appropriate quality controls, its ability to draw causal inferences, the nature of the indicators on which it relies, and finally, its ecological validity. While all these criteria would sound familiar to most scientists, I have barely found philosophical analyses explicitly aimed at emphasizing their role in evidential assessment.  

Diversity of the evidence

 A second important criterion for assessing evidential support, and one more studied in the philosophy of science, is the diversity of the evidence (sometimes called “robustness”; Stegenga & Menon, 2017). 

Within and across scientific disciplines, numerous techniques are used to gather data. While some of these techniques vary in quality, others lie along the same quality level, yet varying in their assumptions and underlying theories. I argue that the more diverse the methodologies used to test a hypothesis, the greater its evidential support.

This criterion is closely connected to the notion of “consilience of inductions”, put forward by William Whewell—one of Darwin’s favorite philosophers—in 1840 (Ruse, 1975). Consilience of inductions refers to the idea that facts of a very different nature can be explained by a single underlying theory. Whewell considered this an indication of a theory’s truth. The idea that I’m putting forward, however, turns the consilience of inductions upside down: While the consilience of inductions starts with data and arrives at theory (thus being inductive), the diversity of evidence thesis starts with a hypothesis and arrives at data (thus being deductive). In other words, rather than devising a theory to account for multifaceted data, the diversity of evidence criterion begins with a hypothesis, looking for different data to test it. 

This is particularly manifest in psychology, where researchers normally conduct more than one experiment to test their theories, with such experiments sometimes being substantially different in their approach. The purpose, in this case, is to rule out alternative interpretations of the results. Likewise, the use of simulation models in evolutionary genetics to rule out the contribution of genetic drift in the evolution of a trait is aimed at providing independent support for a selective hypothesis, which is normally studied through other techniques (such as fitness measurements or molecular indicators). 

How can we measure the diversity of evidence in evolutionary biology? On a rough approximation, three broad classes of methods can be used to test a hypothesis in this discipline: experimental, observational, and model simulations. Within each, several subcategories can be created depending on whether the underlying techniques employed in the experiment, the observational study, or the model simulation come from ecology, developmental biology, molecular biology, comparative biology and phylogeny, population genetics, or behavioral biology. This creates a total of 18 different methods with which to test hypotheses in evolutionary biology. Using a diversity index, such as Shannon’s (H’), it could be possible to measure the diversity of support for a theory or a hypothesis in a systematic way, provided that the method used to test a hypothesis can be unambiguously determined in each paper. This assessment could be useful, for instance, in literature reviews. 

Relative significance

 Finally, the third criterion for evidential assessment is relative significance. This concept, coined by philosopher John Beatty (Beatty, 1997), refers to how many instances of a phenomenon have been described in the literature. This would allow us to evaluate its pervasiveness or generality. According to Beatty, the importance of relative significance stems from evolution’s contingency: given that, to some degree, evolution is stochastic and path-specific, similar processes could have been brought about by different mechanisms. To put a toy example, flight in pterygote insects and birds has been achieved through different anatomical means. So, according to Beatty, instead of worrying about whether a phenomenon applies universally, we must focus on its relative frequency: how common it is compared to other mechanisms.

The fact that relative significance refers to “how many instances have been described in the literature” makes it unsuitable to assess the evidential quality of a single paper. Instead, it is more useful to evaluate a mechanistic hypothesis in general, such as the plasticity-first evolution hypothesis or negative frequency-dependent selection.  

Now, I see a limitation in Beatty’s account of relative significance: I believe that instead of a single unified concept of relative significance, there are at least three of them that are important to evolutionary biologists. The first one would be taxonomic relative significance (TaRS). TaRS refers to how common a phenomenon or mechanism is within and across taxa. For instance, the TaRS of the Weismann barrier might be quite high in eumetazoans (i.e., real animals), but much lower in plants. Similarly, local adaptation through chromosomal inversions might occur more frequently in certain lineages than others. 

The second kind of relative significance is trait relative significance (TrRS), and it amounts to how many traits within a given taxon have evolved through a particular mechanism. This is important, as some traits might be more prone than others to evolve through certain mechanisms. For example, plasticity-first evolution might predominantly affect tissues that display exploratory behavior, such as those making up the vascular and bone systems. (Note: Although partitioning organisms into more or less discrete traits has been widely criticized, two strong arguments, in my view, support it. First, there are successful examples of trait ontologies, such as those cataloging the human or Drosophila phenotypes. This suggests that although it might still not be possible to “cut nature at its joints” phenotypically speaking, the success of these ontologies suggests that these attempts are not completely arbitrary. And second, organisms are built modularly, which means that if they cannot be divided on a trait-by-trait basis, at least they can be done so in a modular fashion.)

Finally, the third kind is evolutionary relative significance (ERS). Not all traits are equally important for adaptation and evolution. ERS aims at assessing to what extent certain mechanisms, prevalent or not on a taxonomic or trait basis, affect those really important characters from the point of the evolutionary history of a taxon. In that sense, for instance, niche construction might not be particularly prevalent when it comes to traits but still affect those characters that have shaped the history of many taxa—such as dam building in beavers.  

Final comments

Wrapping up, in addition to theory assessment, evidence assessment plays a crucial role in science. However, beyond confirmationism, not too much attention has been given to this question in the philosophy of science. In addition to the quantity of evidence for a hypothesis, the quality, diversity, and relative significance of data are important components of evidential support assessment, at least in evolutionary biology. 

Let me finish boldly, like the Wave ecotype of Littorina saxatilis: Kuhn believed that the epistemic values for theory assessment have been espoused by scientists since the Scientific Revolution, thus constituting essential features of science. Their application to specific cases, however, would not be algorithmic, leaving space for differences between individual scientists and epochs. Much like Kuhn, I believe that the above standards of evidence appraisal, broadly construed, have been in place since the beginning of modern science, although their materialization might have varied across times and disciplines. But, again with Kuhn, I believe that their application to specific cases might be to some extent subjective and context dependent. At least until a systematic articulation of these standards is carried out. Such systematic articulation should be the task of future philosophy of science.
