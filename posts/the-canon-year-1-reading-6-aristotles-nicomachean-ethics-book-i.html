The Canon, year 1/reading 6—Aristotle’s Nicomachean Ethics, Book I

The sixth reading of the year has been the first book (roughly equivalent to a chapter) of Aristotle’s Nicomachean Ethics. In this book, Aristotle introduces his approach to ethics. To put it briefly, Aristotle thinks ethics should focus less on isolated actions and more on the kind of person we ought to become in order to achieve eudaimonia—the fullest realization of human nature. This can be puzzling to contemporary readers, since much modern ethical reflection centers around rights and consequences, rather than around character traits. In this sense, Aristotle's ethics can be described as a naturalistic exploration into the psychological roots of human excellence. 

What comes next is not a summary of the first book of the Nicomachean Ethics, but rather a list of considerations by Aristotle that I found interesting to reflect upon. 

  • The precision of ethical reflection: Aristotle claims that ethics cannot deliver precise statements about eudaimonia and the good life. Human life is too messy and complex to provide exact generalizations. For this reason, we ought to be content with propositions and theories that are roughly true, or that apply to a majority of cases only. I think he is correct, and I wonder whether his argument applies to other social sciences and humanistic disciplines, including the philosophy of science. I don’t see Aristotle’s argument, though, as a call against general theories, but rather as a call for epistemic humility. 
  • Teleology: For Aristotle, everything we do aims at some good. The Aristotelian “good” is diverse, unlike Plato’s, and, in a way, hierarchical: some goods are subordinate to others. The maximum good—i.e., that which we do not desire for something else, but rather for itself—is, for Aristotle, happiness or eudaimonia. Aristotle defines eudaimonia as flourishing, or the development of the most outstanding human character traits. I fully agree with Aristotle here. I’m particularly drawn to his conception of happiness, which sees it not so much as a fleeting event but rather as an enduring psychological disposition emerging from the cultivation of good habits.
  • Common opinions about the happy life:  As usual, Aristotle considers the views of the many regarding the happy life. Apart from the life devoted to money, which Aristotle dismisses outright, these are: i) the life of pleasure, ii) the life of honors (i.e., the political life), and iii) the contemplative life. Of these, Aristotle thinks that the life of pleasure surely must not be a happy human life, since other animals can live it—the human good must reside in something that only humans possess. The life of honors is arguably good, since it requires cultivating valuable virtues. But it is not the highest life possible, since it depends on other people, which makes it fragile. A happy life should be as unwavering as it can be, not easily snatched away. The one that is left—the contemplative life—must be, therefore, the best possible human life. Aristotle’s musings on the contemplative life, covered in book X, are one of the most inspiring texts I’ve ever read. But let’s leave them for another occasion. 
  • The definition of eudaimonia: Aristotle defines eudaimonia as an activity of the soul according to virtue—and if there is more than one virtue, according to the highest possible virtue. Let’s briefly unpack this definition. First, happiness is an activity. It would be odd to say that someone is happy while she rests. We rest, in Aristotle’s view, to recover strength to do stuff, and it’s such stuff that constitutes our happiness. Second, to define human happiness, we need to know what’s specific about humans, biologically speaking. This connects to his criticism of the life of pleasure. In Aristotle’s biology, the soul, that is, the set of processes that characterize living beings, differs between kinds of organisms. Humans differ from plants, which only metabolize, and from non-human animals, which metabolize and perceive, in being able to reason. Thus, the activity of the soul that is typical of the happy human must be one that conforms to reason. Third, the rational activity that characterizes happiness must, in order to produce happiness or eudaimonia, be carried out virtuously, or with excellence. And if there is more than one virtue—which, for Aristotle, there is—according to the most important ones. Such virtues, Aristotle informs us in later chapters, are the intellectual virtues—that is, those needed to excel in the contemplative life.
  • The role of externals: The final consideration I want to mention concerns Aristotle’s observation that the happy life partly relies on external goods. We need others to live happily—only beasts and gods can afford to flourish on their own. Unlike the Stoics, who would argue that the wise man can be happy while being imprisoned or tortured, Aristotle thinks external goods are necessary. It’s difficult to be completely happy, he argues, if your children die, or if they are bad, or if you are poor and cannot afford to eat well. What’s more, you’ll have trouble achieving happiness if you’re very ugly, Aristotle claims. I wish the Stoics were right here, and that we could be completely immune to external events, but that’s unfortunately not the case. That said, the differences between the Stoics and Aristotle should not be exaggerated either. As the skeptical philosopher Carneades used to say, Stoics and Peripatetics taught the same ideas using different terminology (Rist, 1977). Although perhaps an exaggeration, there is a kernel of truth in this view. For example, Aristotle argues that the virtuous person faces adversity with magnanimity (megalopsychia) and good temper, and that, despite externals, he would do everything he can to ensure his happiness does not entirely depend on fortune. 

These are just five themes or ideas of the many put forward in the first book of the Nicomachean Ethics. A book, by the way, which only spans around 30 pages. Imagine the breadth and scope of the entire set of books that comprise the treatise. I cannot help but profoundly admire the quality and originality of Aristotle’s reflections, which are as thought-provoking today as they were 2,300 years ago.
