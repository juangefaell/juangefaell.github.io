Should We Really Aim for Consensus?: Comments on Beatty and Moore (2010)

Consensus is considered by many, scientists, philosophers and laypeople alike, a defining characteristic of science. Inasmuch as the scientific enterprise is driven by empirical data and a broadly shared set of epistemic values, consensus is its expected outcome. However, on many occasions, scientists show substantial disagreement. Although in most cases these disagreements are easily resolved when new empirical data is gathered, in some specific circumstances, and for various reasons, empirical data is not sufficient to resolve the discrepancies. At least in those occasions, therefore, consensus may not be the ending point of scientific inquiry. 


What lessons to draw from this latter kind of disagreement is a fascinating problem in the philosophy of science. Broadly speaking, we are left with two options: either we find a way to resolve such deep disagreements, or we give up the idea of consensus in science altogether. A paper that I’ve read this week seems to lean towards the latter possibility (well, sort of). This is John Beatty and Alfred Moore’s Should we aim for consensus?, published in 2010 in the journal Episteme. 

This is a very interesting paper, if only because it is written jointly by a philosopher of science (Beatty) and a political philosopher (Moore) –which is not very common, as far as I know. Besides, John Beatty is one of the leading contemporary philosophers of biology, having done some really, really fascinating work on the philosophy of evolutionary biology. In this paper, Beatty and Moore (henceforth B&M) try to show why we should not consider consensus a worthy epistemic goal of science; or at least, not its main goal, regardless of how it is achieved or at the expense of other values. In my opinion, the paper is well argued and the main thesis defended sounds quite convincing. However, I found it a little bit unstructured and repetitive at times, something which may have been easily avoided dividing the paper in clear and straightforward sections. 

In the following, as usual, I will make a brief personal reconstruction of the paper, highlighting the aspects I found more interesting to understand the current MS versus EES controversy. 

What Exactly is Consensus and Why is It Not Good?

A sensible way to begin discussing the role of consensus in science and other areas of rational inquiry is to try to figure out what is exactly consensus, or what it amounts to. In their paper, B&M assume from the outset that consensus equals unanimity. Unanimity means that, in an assembly of people, everyone holds exactly the same opinion on a particular subject matter.

This definition of consensus directly points to the reason why it is not good, according to B&M: if an assembly of people unanimously holds a certain position on a particular issue, chances are high that this position does not really represent the beliefs of its particular members. People tend to disagree with each other on a large number of things; faced with a given problem, be it political, judicial of scientific, there is always more than one sensible or rational alternative at hand. The infamous TINA (“there is no alternative”), popularized by Margaret Thatcher, is not simply true. So, from that very basic fact, it follows that we should be wary of consensus, since it is very likely that in order to achieve total unanimity, some voices have to be silenced or there has been no real deliberation. 

Although some people might agree that this is certainly the case for politics, others might be more reluctant to accept that the situation described above is also true for science as well. As we described in the first paragraph, many people believe that consensus is a key feature of science. However, B&M argue that total unanimity is also very rare in science, basically because science is value-laden and theories are undetermined by evidence (that is, for a given set of data, there is always more than one hypothesis that can account for it). From these two observations, it follows that what applies to political discussions also holds true for scientific debates. 

The Key to a Good Deliberation

In the political philosophy literature, consensus has been one of the main concepts of the deliberative democracy tradition. According to the classical thinkers of this school of thought, and in particular Jürgen Habermas (1984, 1987), consensus should be the end point of rational deliberation when properly conducted. If a given group of people with different interests and agendas exchange reasons openly and frankly, then it is likely that a consensus will eventually be reached. According to Habermas and other classic deliberation theorists, deliberation should aim for consensus. 

According to B&M, the view put forward by Habermas and their followers is both wrong and harmful; deliberation should not always lead to consensus. If we take seriously the observation that there may be more than one rational course of action, theory or hypothesis, then consensus should not be the ending point of a properly conducted deliberation. Perhaps there would be some situations in which the end point of deliberation would instead be rational disagreement and mutual recognition of the rationality of each option. For B&M, classical deliberation theorists where too monists (to use Isaiah Berlin’s terminology), thinking that there is only one view of the common good that everyone must agree on. 

To avoid the pitfalls of classic deliberation theorists, B&M note that the new wave of political philosophers interested in deliberation have abandoned consensus and instead have placed an emphasis on the quality of deliberation, regardless of whether or not it leads to consensus. 

But what constitutes a good deliberation, according to modern deliberation theorists? Here B&M also draw on classic thinkers, including John Stuart Mill, and argue that at least two conditions must be met for a deliberation to be considered truly good: openness and equality. Let me explain.

On the one hand, by openness B&M refer to the existence of a real debate between different alternatives. A given assembly of people is considered to be deliberating well if its contituent members can have the opportunity to freely express their own ideas on the subject matter at hand. The group must be really open and willing to discuss different possibilities, and take them seriously. On the other hand, by equality B&M mean that all members of the assembly are treated as peers. No individual or group of people has prevalence over the rest of the members. 

In short, the real pressing issue is not how to achieve consensus, but how to maintain the quality of deliberations, and that requires us to be open to different ideas, as well as ensuring equality in our treatment of all members of the deliberation process. If these two conditions are met, then we can confidently say that there has been a good deliberation, regardless of whether we have attained consensus or not. 

Openness and equality give us a further reason to doubt the value of consensus, according to B&M. Inasmuch as consensus prioritizes unanimity at all costs, there is always the risk that not all members are treated equal, in addition to not being truly open to discussing different ideas. 

Deliberative Acceptance

So, it seems like we should drop the idea of consensus altogether. But should we? Is there not a kernel of truth in the idea that we should aim for an agreement between different people? B&M seem to think that although consensus may be too demanding and strict a notion, we should still try to reach some basic agreements between epistemic pairs. So perhaps the idea of consensus can be replaced by a notion of group agreement that does not require individual unanimity. To do this, the authors introduce Margaret Gilbert’s notion of “joint acceptance”, according to which: “A group jointly accepts p if and only if the individual members have openly agreed to let p stand as the position of the group” (206). 

That is, in joint acceptance, all members agree that a given statement represents the position of the group as a whole, even if some of these members may personally disagree with p. This notion allows us to maintain agreement while at the same time leave room for personal disagreement, avoiding unanimity. 

Now, one could argue that the disagreeing individuals in a situation of joint acceptance may accept the group’s position for the wrong reasons: perhaps they have been forced to do so, or have simply been ignored by the majority. This is why B&M return to the qualities of a good deliberation to formulate a slightly improved version of Gilbert’s joint acceptance, which they call “deliberative acceptance”: “A group deliberately accepts p if and only if the individual members, based on the quality of their deliberation, have openly agreed to let p stand as the position of the group” (209).

That is, in deliberative acceptance people agree that p remains the position of the whole group precisely because the deliberative process that has led to it meets the criteria of openness and equality. This implies the existence of a sort of consensus, but one which does not amount to unanimity on substantial issues, but on the quality of the procedures: everyone agrees that the deliberation process has met certain criteria of quality. 

In short, deliberate acceptance can be considered a sensible alternative to consensus understood as unanimity, since it allows for individual discrepancy but preserves the value of group agreement. It is deliberate acceptance, not consensus, that can successfully protect the authority of a given social group, whether they be juries, politicians or scientists. So instead of aiming at consensus, we should, at least according to B&M, aim for deliberate acceptance. 

I tend to agree with B&M’s arguments. However, I see an apparent problem with their position. They define deliberate acceptance as a kind of procedural consensus, because what really counts in their framework is not consensus on substantial issues but consensus on how things should proceed. More specifically, there should be consensus on the importance of openness and equality for deliberative processes. The problem with this view, however, is that I fail to see how to reach procedural consensus without any kind of substantive consensus. For instance, in this particular case, it seems to me quite obvious that in order to value openness and equality as characteristics of a good deliberative process, we must also value tolerance and equity. If a given individual does not value tolerance or equity, I do not see how she might be able to defend openness and equality as preconditions for a good deliberation. The problem is that agreeing on the value of tolerance and equity is clearly a substantial consensus.

In sum, even the most basic forms of procedural consensus seem to rely on some kind of substantive consensus. So maybe we shouldn’t so easily abandon the idea of substantive consensus. Don’t you agree?
