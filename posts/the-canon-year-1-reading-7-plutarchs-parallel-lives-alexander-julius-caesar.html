The Canon, year 1/reading 7—Plutarch’s Parallel Lives: Alexander-Julius Caesar

The seventh reading of the year was Plutarch’s Parallel Lives: Alexander-Julius Caesar. The Parallel Lives of Plutarch are a series of books describing or comparing famous characters from ancient Greece and Rome. Being Plutarch a Greek living under Roman rule and writing for both Greek and Roman audiences, one of his main aims was to stress the similarities between notable Greeks and Romans. Yet, rather than conventional histories, Plutarch’s Parallel Lives are collections of anecdotes from the lives of the two characters compared. And within these anecdotes, Plutarch describes not only the most remarkable, but also the most apparently insignificant, since for the author:

“(…) the most glorious exploits do not always furnish us with the clearest discoveries of virtue or vice in men; sometimes a matter of less moment, an expression or a jest, informs us better of their character and inclinations, than the most famous sieges, the greatest armaments, or the bloodiest battles whatsoever.” 

I’ve enjoyed the Parallel Lives, although I must admit that it was a dry reading at moments. I’m not very familiar with the history of ancient Greece and Rome, so it was hard for me to follow or fully grasp the implications of some actions described by Plutarch. The notes included in the book sometimes helped, but there were many times in which they were not sufficient, since they presupposed some acquaintance with the topic. 

Despite this, I was super happy to find many well-known anecdotes in the book. In the case of Alexander, for example, Plutarch narrates his relationship with Aristotle, who, according to the author, instilled in him the love of literature (Alexander kept a copy of the Iliad under his pillow) and with whom his relationship gradually deteriorated. He also recounts his famous interaction with Diogenes the Cynic, where, after being asked by Alexander what he wanted from him, Diogenes responded that all he needed was for Alexander to move so as not to stand between him and the sun. That response struck Alexander so much that he allegedly said that if he were not Alexander, he would desire to be Diogenes. 

Plutarch also describes the many battles and conquests that Alexander carried out, although almost always from a personal point of view. He describes how, for example, he slept like a baby before an important battle against the Persian army, led by Darius III, king of Persia, or how he managed to overcome very numerous armies through a combination of strategy and boldness. 

The book depicts how Alexander eventually made it to India and how he was only stopped from continuing his conquests by his own army. It was also interesting to know that, as he progressed in his invasions, Alexander came to adopt many local dressing customs, which he would mix with his own Greek-Macedonian outfits. Also particularly moving is Plutarch’s description of Alexander’s relationship with Bucephalus, his horse, who passed away towards the end of Alexander’s life, either because of the wounds he got in the battlefield, or from old age and exhaustion, depending on the source. 

As for Caesar, the book also contains many interesting anecdotes. It mentions, for example, his various conflicts with Cicero and Cato the Younger, senators of the Republic, followers of stoicism (although Cato more than Cicero), and opponents to Caesar’s increasingly growing power. Plutarch also gives testimony of Caesar’s ability to win the support of the masses (for instance, by putting forward different social policy measures), and of destroying his political rivals. He also mentions his military excellence, which he demonstrated in the Gallic Wars, as well as the various milestones in the Civil War that led to his election as perpetual dictator. These include, for instance, the crossing of the Rubicon River, where he allegedly uttered the words Alea iacta est (“the die has been cast”), or his affair with Cleopatra during his stay in Egypt. 

Plutarch’s biography of Caesar also describes the plot to kill him, led by Cassius and Brutus. Knowing that Shakespeare took inspiration from Plutarch’s Parallel Lives to write Julius Caesar, I watched Joseph L. Mankiewicz’s adaptation of Shakespeare’s tragedy before reading this part of the book. This helped me understand much better the chain of events that led to his assassination. 

Based on the anecdotes described in the book, it does not surprise me that Plutarch saw a parallelism between Alexander and Caesar. Both were extremely ambitious men, moved by an insatiable will for glory that prevented them to experience any kind of contentment with what they had already achieved. As Plutarch puts it referring to Caesar: 

“(…) the many noble exploits he had done did not serve as an inducement to him to sit still and reap the fruit of his past labors, but were incentives and encouragements to go on, and raised in him ideas of still greater actions, and a desire of new glory, as if the present were all spent. It was in fact a sort of emulous struggle with himself, as it had been with another, how he might outdo his past actions by his future.”

This boundless ambition led both to commit atrocities and to be merciless towards their enemies. Yet, both showed magnanimity when occasion demanded. In this sense, Plutarch conveys very well the complex psychological nature of these two characters, who could be able to do whatever it took to satisfy their desire for greatness, but who also carried out noble actions. Plutarch approvingly mentions Alexander’s extreme respect for the kings he defeated in battle, as well as how, despite destroying all checks and balances, Caesar passed important legislation, including a very successful calendar reform. 

Yet, unfortunately, Plutarch’s explicit comparison of Alexander and Caesar’s psychologies has been lost, so the task is left for the reader to undertake.
