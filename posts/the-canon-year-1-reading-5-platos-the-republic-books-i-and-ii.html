The Canon, year 1/reading 5—Plato’s The Republic, Books I and II

The fifth reading of this first year was the first and second books of Plato’s The Republic. This is such a superb reading, with so many interesting topics covered, that it’s hard for me to recap. Book I commences with Socrates and Glaucon returning from a celebration at the Piraeus, the main port of Athens. On their way back, they encounter Polemarchus, who, after a brief chat, invites them to their place. Once there, Socrates first establishes a dialogue with Cephalus, Polemarchus’ father. The conversation begins with Socrates asking Cephalus what life is like at old age. In a recurrent theme in classic literature (e.g., Sophocles, but also Seneca and Cicero afterwards), Cephalus replies that, despite popular opinion, old age is pleasurable because all the disruptive passions of youth, which cause people terrible suffering, are mostly gone. 

Then the conversation evolves, and Polemarchus jumps in to discuss the nature of justice. The core of this part of the dialogue is Socrates refuting Polemarchus’s attempts at defining justice. First, Socrates tackles the view that justice is giving people what is theirs, a view that he rebuts by pointing out that it would not be fair to return a borrowed knife to someone who has gone mad and could hurt himself or others. Second, Socrates addresses the view that justice is treating friends well and foes badly. This is wrong, according to Socrates, since treating someone badly, even if he deserves it, is by necessity unjust—doing harm is always immoral. 

Once Socrates refutes Polemarchus, Thrasymachus enters the scene. This is where the dialogue gets most interesting. Thrasymachus defends the view that justice is what is good for the stronger, particularly the ruler, which entails justifying whatever he does. Socrates, however, notes that rulers sometimes make mistakes, hurting themselves while pursuing their own interests. This invalidates the view that justice is whatever the stronger does. Thrasymachus, nevertheless, is not persuaded, responding that the true ruler cannot make mistakes. This forces Socrates to change his line of attack, asserting that it is a contradiction that someone rules for his own benefit—a just sovereign always governs for the good of their subordinates. 

Then, the conversation continues to evolve, and Thrasymachus passes from trying to define justice to arguing why injustice is better than justice. The core of his argument is that injustice is preferable because unjust people, if they manage to get away with it, always end up better off than just persons. 

Thrasymachus pronounces a long speech, but eventually Glaucon takes over as Socrates’ main interlocutor. To defend the just life, Socrates appeals to wisdom, harmony, and the good consequences they bring, but he fails to convince Glaucon. Glaucon replies that, to make the case for justice, Socrates must provide arguments that are independent of the good consequences it allegedly brings. That is, he must convince him of why, on the one hand, someone would prefer justice over injustice if, by being just, he ends up being badly treated, and, on the other hand, why someone would not prefer injustice if this results in his material success. 

To illustrate this, Glaucon introduces a classic in the history of philosophy: Gyges’ ring. I didn’t know this story was exposed in The Republic, so it was great to encounter it. In the story, Gyges, a poor shepherd, finds a ring that turns him invisible. After this, he starts committing injustices, which increases his wealth and allows him to become king. Glaucon asks Socrates why, if we were to find ourselves in the same situation as Gyges, we should prefer justice over injustice. 

If you were thinking, as I, that Socrates would provide an answer to such a puzzle, you’d be disappointed. Rather, Socrates invites Glaucon to consider justice in the city. His justification for switching topics is the following: deliberating about justice requires good eyesight, which, according to Socrates, neither he nor Glaucon has. So, what do people do when, due to insufficient visual acuity, cannot see what a certain sign says? They look for places where the same message is reproduced at a greater scale. In the case of justice, this is the city, whose fairness resembles that of individuals in various respects.

Therefore, Socrates starts exploring the justice of the city. He begins by outlining how and why societies emerge. Basically, this occurs because individuals are not self-sufficient, relying on others to obtain the things they need to survive. This leads to specialization and the emergence of occupations. As people’s needs increase, societies expand, resulting in new occupations. But expansion requires more territory, so when societies reach a certain level of complexity, they develop a warrior class that fights for new territory and defends their own. Considering how crucial warriors are for society, Socrates argues it is them who should rule.

Here is when things start to go wrong. Until that moment, Plato’s Socrates appeared as a staunch advocate for justice. What was not obvious for me was that the societal counterpart to his view of justice is a military-religious dictatorship in which a cast of warrior-philosophers decides what is good and what isn’t. It is they who determine, for instance, that poets such as Homer and Hesiod should be banned from the city since they offer a false picture of the gods—a picture that would ultimately be detrimental to the moral development of those who are destined to rule the city. This is how the dialogue ends, only to be continued in Book III with the introduction of the “noble lie” doctrine, which fits perfectly with what has been argued in the last part of Book II. 

In sum, thus, my feeling reading the first two books of Plato’s Republic is that I was fully on board with Socrates’ defense of justice until he started outlining what he meant by a just society. At that point, it became clear to me why Plato has been deemed as one of the fiercest critics of liberal democracy, as well as the inspiration for authoritarian regimes of all stripes—including modern “communist” China. 

Be that as it may, this was a fascinating text that I’ve really enjoyed reading.
