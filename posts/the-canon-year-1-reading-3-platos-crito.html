The Canon, year 1/reading 3—Plato’s Crito

This week, I read Plato’s Crito. This is his shortest dialogue, spanning only around 30 pages. It recounts an alleged conversation between Socrates, in jail awaiting execution, and Crito, one of his most faithful pupils and friends. The dialogue commences with Crito arriving at the jail very early in the morning. Rather than awaking Socrates, he stares at him while he sleeps placidly, a sign that, despite knowing he will die soon, Socrates is peaceful and in a good mood. Once he wakes up, Crito informs Socrates that he has plotted a plan to free him from prison and avoid death. Knowing that Socrates will be reluctant, Crito tries to persuade him by putting forward several arguments, none of which convince Socrates that he must break the sentence issued by the Athenian assembly. 

In a nutshell, Crito argues that if he doesn’t break out, people will think that he and other pupils are bad friends, because, having the possibility of helping their master, they refused to do so. In addition to that, he points out that Socrates’ child will remain fatherless, with the ensuing negative consequences this would have for him. 

As said, Socrates is not particularly compelled by Crito’s arguments and tries to refute them right away. His counterargument is structured as follows. First, Socrates tackles Crito’s worry that people might think he is a bad friend. He asserts that the majority’s opinion is worthless and that we should only pay attention to the views of those who know: in the same way as the athlete does not pay attention to ordinary people to decide what to eat or how to train, but only to those who know about nutrition and sports, we should only consider the opinions of those who are knowledgeable about morality. To Socrates, how popular a view is does not determine its truthfulness. For different reasons, the majority tends to get moral judgments wrong, and only a minority of enlightened individuals can effectively tell right from wrong. And to that instructed minority, avoiding a sentence issued by a legitimate body is always an injustice. 

And this connects to Socrates’ second point: you should never respond to an injustice with another injustice. As he emphasizes in other dialogues, Socrates believes it’s better to suffer an injustice than to commit it. This should prevent us from being unjust even to those who had been unjust to us, as responding with an injustice to an injustice only augments the net number of injustices in the world. But why is eluding a sentence an injustice, we might wonder? To explain why, Socrates imagines a hypothetical conversation with the Laws themselves. 

The Laws remark that every individual is as much a product of the laws of their city as they are of their parents: the city (or the state, in today’s terms), provides many of the needed resources to become a fully functioning citizen, from education to nourishment. As such, we owe our cities and the laws that rule them the same kind of respect we owe to our parents. If we don’t like a city or its laws, we can either leave or persuade those in charge to modify what we don’t like, using arguments and not brute force. Socrates, the Laws argue, has remained all his life in Athens, despite having numerous opportunities to move elsewhere, showing that he likes Athens and its laws. Socrates tried to persuade those in charge not to sentence him, but he proved unsuccessful, so the only thing that remains is to obey. If you comply with the laws when they benefit you, you should observe them when they rule against your interests. Doing otherwise would amount to trying to undermine the legitimacy of the laws and being incoherent, which, to Socrates, is unacceptable. That’s the reason why he must not escape.

Crito, disappointed, contends that his master is right, thereby refusing to convince him to break out of prison. And that’s how the dialogue ends. 

I like Socrates’ argument that we should only pay attention to those who know, and that we should try to break free from the chains of public opinion when it is not substantiated by compelling arguments. I’m also sympathetic to the view that the common good is more important than our personal interests, and that, therefore, we should comply with the rules of the system that has nourished us in so many ways. After all, I do not envision the existence of rights without duties. However, I experience cognitive dissonance because I’m aware that the above principle can sometimes clash with certain basic liberal rights that I esteem, and that, in my view, no legislative or judicial body should be able to take away from individuals. For this reason, Socrates’ unwavering commitment to the common good, even over his own life, got me thinking. For the moment, I see no other compromise between the two than classic civic republicanism, which tries to combine the basic structure of liberal rights with a more demanding conception of the obligations of individuals towards their fellow citizens.
